# Ethics 26: honest claims, the duty to migrate, export controls and access — explained

## The big picture

The technical lessons produced three kinds of numbers that carry ethical weight. "Benchmarking honestly against classical solvers" showed how easily a benchmark manufactures a quantum win. "Resource estimation: logical vs physical qubits and feasibility" put RSA-2048 at about 9 million physical qubits in our model, while the cited 2025 estimate is under a million. "Quantum and post-quantum: crypto inventory and migration plan" turned Mosca's inequality into code that sets migration priorities. Ethics here is not a separate subject: it is what you do with those numbers when someone else is relying on you. This lesson gives one case (the QuantaNova press release and three readers who act on it) and asks you to reason in a structured, defensible way.

There are no theorems to prove. Instead you apply a named framework step by step, then test the result against a view that weighs things differently.

## Worked analysis: the ACM Code of Ethics (2018), step by step

**Step 1: establish the facts and separate their kinds.** *Why first:* every principle below depends on what is actually known. Measured: a sampling task ran on 120 qubits. Baseline: a single-CPU brute-force simulator, not the best classical method. Extrapolation: "break RSA-2048 by 2029", which a sampling result does not support (RSA needs Shor with error correction; lesson 19). Unsupported: "encryption as we know it is obsolete" (symmetric crypto and FIPS 203 ML-KEM exist).

**Step 2: list stakeholders and what each stands to lose.** *Why:* ACM 1.2 (avoid harm) is about people, not abstractions. Patients lose long-lived confidentiality; the hospital board can waste a budget; students may be excluded or exposed to legal risk; under-resourced universities may be locked out; the profession loses credibility to hype.

**Step 3: apply the honesty principles to the claim.** ACM 1.3 (be honest and trustworthy) and 2.5 (comprehensive evaluations including risks), with IEEE item 5 ("realistic in stating claims"). *Why these:* the release is a claim about capability. Verdict: the advantage claim is unproven until compared against the best classical method on equal budgets (our lesson 18 harness is exactly that), and the RSA date is hype. ACM 2.7 (foster public awareness) means saying so specifically, not amplifying it.

**Step 4: apply harm avoidance to the hospital.** ACM 1.2 and 3.7 (special care for infrastructure). *Why:* health records are societal infrastructure. The risk is real for a reason that has nothing to do with QuantaNova: with `X = 25` years of confidentiality, harvest-now-decrypt-later already threatens data sent today. The ethical response is a *planned* migration (inventory and plan by 2028 per NCSC, hybrid ML-KEM for the longest-lived flows, PQC roadmaps in procurement, NIST IR 8547's 2030/2035 dates), not an emergency purchase from a partner of the hype source, which is a conflict of interest to disclose.

**Step 5: apply rules and fairness to the lab.** ACM 2.3 (know and respect existing rules) and 1.4 (be fair, do not discriminate). *Why both:* they pull in different directions. The BIS rule and the EU dual-use regulation may cover technology transfer to a foreign processor; publication may fall under the public-domain or basic-research exclusions. Action: involve the compliance office before sharing; do not exclude students by nationality pre-emptively; challenge over-broad rules through channels.

**Step 6: apply fairness to the agency.** ACM 1.4 and 3.7, plus UN A/RES/78/287 on capacity-building. *Why:* access decisions decide who can learn the field. Fund access with open-source toolchains (this course runs entirely on them), transparent allocation, and measurement of who actually uses it.

**Step 7: resolve the conflicts explicitly.** Honesty vs commercial pressure: honesty wins (1.3 is not negotiable). Openness vs export law: comply, use the exclusions, challenge through channels. Urgency vs proportionality: plan to official timelines, spend where data lifetime is longest. The public good (ACM 3.1) is the tie-breaker.

## A contrasting view: security-first precaution

A security-first analyst weighs worst-case harm above expected harm. On the hospital, they would accept the "emergency" framing: if a credible actor might decrypt 25-year records, the cost of acting early is small next to the cost of being wrong, so they would accelerate spending now even with an unproven vendor. On the lab, they would treat any doubt about export scope as a reason not to share, accepting slower science as the price of security.

Where this view is strong: it takes tail risk seriously, and Mosca's inequality is itself a precautionary argument. Where it fails the ACM analysis: precaution does not require buying from the source of the hype (step 4), and blanket non-sharing has a fairness cost that falls on specific students (step 5). The two views agree on *what* to do for the hospital (migrate) and disagree on *how fast and through whom*. Writing down that disagreement, rather than hiding it, is the professional move.

## Worked problems

**P1.** `X = 10`, `Y = 6`, `Z = 12`. At risk? Then `Y = 2`.
1. Add `X + Y = 16`. *Why:* data sent today must stay secret for X years, and you keep sending it for Y more years until migration ends.
2. Compare with `Z = 12`: `16 > 12`, exposed for `16 − 12 = 4` years. *Why:* that is how long protected data outlives the arrival of a CRQC.
3. With `Y = 2`: `X + Y = 12`, not greater than 12: margin 0, borderline. *Why:* shortening migration is the only lever the organisation controls; crypto agility (lesson 21) shortens Y.

**P6.** Google's 2019 estimate: 10,000 years classical vs 200 s quantum; a 2022 computation took about 15 hours on 512 GPUs.
1. Convert: `10,000 × 365.25 × 24 = 87,660,000` hours. *Why:* compare like with like.
2. Divide: `87,660,000 / 15 = 5,844,000`. *Why:* the classical estimate shrank about 5.8 million times.
3. `15 h = 54,000 s = 270 ×` the quantum run. *Why:* advantage is relative to the best classical method *known at the time*, and that target moves. This is lesson 18's rule, applied to history.

## Common confusions

- **Q:** If the claim is hype, the hospital can relax. — **A:** HNDL with `X = 25` makes the risk real regardless of QuantaNova; only the *urgency framing* is wrong.
- **Q:** Export controls mean you cannot publish. — **A:** Public-domain and basic-research exclusions exist; ask compliance before deciding.
- **Q:** Ethics is opinion, so any answer is fine. — **A:** A defensible answer names principles, applies them to facts, and states how conflicts were resolved.
