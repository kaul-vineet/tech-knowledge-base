# QAOA on MaxCut vs the classical optimum — explained

## The intuition

MaxCut: colour each node black or white so that as many edges as possible join different colours. Each bitstring is a colouring; its *cut* counts split edges. With 5 nodes brute force checks all 32 strings, so you always know the right answer.

QAOA is a parameterised circuit (lesson 10) with two alternating layers. The **cost layer** gives each bitstring a phase proportional to its cut, `e^{−iγ·cut}`. The **mixer layer** (`RX(2β)` on every qubit) lets amplitude flow between strings that differ by one bit, so the phases turn into interference that favours good cuts. Repeat the pair `p` times. A classical optimiser tunes the `2p` angles to maximise the expected cut `⟨C⟩`. The score is the *approximation ratio* `⟨C⟩ / optimum`.

The hard part is the classical optimisation: the angle landscape has several local minima.

**Analogy:** hill-climbing in fog over a landscape with many peaks: you reach the nearest summit, not necessarily the highest.
Where the analogy breaks: each "height reading" is itself a quantum expectation estimated from shots (exact here, noisy on hardware), so even the slope is uncertain.

```text
|+>^5 --[cost: phase e^{-i gamma cut}]--[mixer: RX(2 beta) each]-- x p --> measure
          ^ gamma, beta from classical optimiser (minimise -<C>)
reference: brute force over 32 strings -> max cut 5
score: ratio = <C>/5 ;  p=1 grid -> 0.8188, descent 0.822 ; p=2 warm start -> 0.9247
```

The one thing to remember: QAOA quality depends on where the optimiser starts as much as on the circuit depth `p`.

## Walkthrough: what actually happens when you run it

1. **You run** `python qaoa_lab.py`.
   **What happens inside:** `CUT` holds all 32 cut sizes; `qaoa_state` applies the diagonal cost phase and `RX(2β)` per qubit; `landscape()` scans a 41 × 21 grid; `optimize()` is central-difference descent; `solve(2, seed=0)` starts p = 2 from one random point.
   **What you see:**
   ```text
   brute force: max cut 5 by ['01001', '01011', '10100', '10110']
   {"p": 1, "method": "grid 41x21", "gamma": 0.6283, "beta": 0.3927, "ratio": 0.8188}
   {"p": 1, "method": "grid + descent", "ratio": 0.822}
   {"p": 2, "method": "random start seed 0", "ratio": 0.7002, "params": [2.067, 0.909, -0.493, 0.029]}
   most likely bitstring 01101 (cut 4), P(any optimal cut) = 0.1137
   ```
   **What it means:** four optimal strings (two colourings and their colour-swaps). At p = 1 the grid finds a good basin and descent polishes it to 0.822. The p = 2 run is *worse* than p = 1, although a p = 2 circuit can do everything p = 1 can (set the second layer to zero). That is an optimiser failure, not a circuit limit.

2. **You run** `python try_it.py`.
   **What you see:**
   ```text
   random_starts     {'ratios': [0.7002, 0.8352, 0.9247, 0.9247, 0.7009, 0.8329, 0.7002, 0.8329], 'hits': 2}
   sample_the_answer {'P_optimal': 0.707, 'best_sampled_cut': 5, 'optimal_samples': 13}
   ```
   **What it means:** only 2 of 8 random starts reach the 0.9247 basin; the rest land in three other local minima. At the good parameters an optimal cut has probability 0.707, so 13 of 20 shots are optimal: in practice you keep the best *sample*, and check it classically.

## Failure lab: a deeper circuit that scores worse

1. **Inject:** `solve(2, seed=0)`: one random start in `[0, π)^4`, then local descent.
2. **Symptom:** ratio 0.7002 (line 4 above), below the p = 1 grid's 0.8188, and `P(any optimal cut) = 0.1137`.
3. **Diagnose:** the descent converged (more steps do not move it), so the gradient is zero: a local minimum. `try_it` confirms that the landscape has several basins and that the start decides which one you get.
4. **Fix:** `solution_restarts.py` warm-starts p = 2 from the p = 1 grid optimum, interpolated to `[γ/2, γ, β, β/2]`, adds 3 seeded random restarts, and keeps the best.
5. **Proof:**
   ```text
   $ BUILD_TASK_MODULE=qaoa_lab python -m unittest test_build_task
   AssertionError: 0.6997 not greater than or equal to 0.82
   AssertionError: 0.7002 not greater than or equal to 0.92
   FAILED (failures=2)
   $ python -m unittest test_build_task
   Ran 3 tests in 25.684s
   OK
   ```
   A third test demands p = 2 ≥ p = 1: "more depth must not hurt" as a regression check.

## Common confusions

- **Q:** A ratio of 0.92 means 92% of shots are optimal. — **A:** It is an average cut; `P_optimal` is 0.707 at those parameters.
- **Q:** Higher `p` always helps. — **A:** Only with a good optimiser: the naive p = 2 run gives 0.7002.
- **Q:** QAOA beats brute force here. — **A:** Brute force checks 32 strings exactly; the lab uses it as ground truth. Lesson 18 makes the comparison fair.
