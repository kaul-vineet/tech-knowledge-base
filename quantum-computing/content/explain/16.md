# A quantum classifier vs a classical baseline — explained

## The intuition

A variational quantum classifier is a tiny neural network whose layers are rotations. Two features become rotation angles on two qubits (*angle encoding*); trainable `RY(w)` layers plus a CNOT chain follow; the sign of `⟨Z0⟩ + b` is the class. Each weight's gradient comes from two runs at `w ± π/2` (the *parameter-shift rule*), exact rather than a finite difference.

The lab asks two honest questions. First: does it beat a classical model with a similar parameter count on the same split? A 3-parameter logistic regression is the bar. Second: does it still train when you make it bigger? For random initial angles, the gradients of wide, deep circuits shrink toward zero (a *barren plateau*). On hardware every gradient is estimated from shots, so once the true gradient is smaller than the shot noise, training becomes a random walk.

**Analogy:** a deep network with bad weight initialisation: vanishing gradients, so nothing learns.
Where the analogy breaks: random circuits spread information over an exponentially large space, and reading tiny gradients precisely costs many more shots.

```text
x=(x1,x2) --RY(x1),RY(x2)--> [RY(w) per qubit + CNOT chain] x L --> <Z0> + b --> sign -> class
gradient: parameter shift  d<Z0>/dw = (f(w+pi/2) - f(w-pi/2)) / 2
trainability check: median |grad| over 20 random inits  vs  shot floor 0.0224 (1000 shots)
```

The one thing to remember: measure gradient size against shot noise before you scale a variational model, and always compare with a classical baseline on the same split.

## Walkthrough: what actually happens when you run it

1. **You run** `python classifier_lab.py`.
   **What happens inside:** `make_dataset(seed=3)` splits 40 train / 20 test before training; `train_quantum` runs 60 epochs and logs one JSON line each; `logistic_baseline` runs 500 steps; `gradient_scan` measures `|∂⟨Z0⟩/∂w[0,0]|` over 20 random inits per size.
   **What you see:**
   ```text
   {"model": "quantum 2q x 2 layers (5 params)", "train_acc": 0.975, "test_acc": 0.9}
   {"model": "logistic regression (3 params)", "train_acc": 0.975, "test_acc": 0.85}
   test accuracy over 5 dataset seeds (quantum, classical): [(1.0, 1.0), (0.95, 0.95), (0.95, 0.95), (0.9, 0.85), (0.85, 0.8)]
   shot-noise floor for one gradient at 1000 shots: 0.0224
   {"n": 2, "layers": 2, "readout": "local", "median_abs_grad": 0.7171, "below_shot_floor": 0.0}
   {"n": 6, "layers": 12, "readout": "local", "median_abs_grad": 0.1121, "below_shot_floor": 0.1}
   {"n": 10, "layers": 20, "readout": "local", "median_abs_grad": 0.0208, "below_shot_floor": 0.55}
   ```
   **What it means:** on seed 3 the quantum model wins by one test point of 20; across 5 seeds it ties three times and leads by one point twice: a tie, not an advantage. Meanwhile the median gradient falls 0.7171 → 0.0208 as the circuit grows.

2. **You run** `sed -n '1p;60p' logs/train_2q_2l.jsonl`.
   **What you see:**
   ```text
   {"epoch": 0, "loss": 2.0398, "train_acc": 0.425, "grad_norm": 0.5546}
   {"epoch": 59, "loss": 0.2661, "train_acc": 0.975, "grad_norm": 0.0116}
   ```
   **What it means:** healthy training: the gradient shrinks because the model converged.

3. **You run** `python try_it.py`.
   **What you see:**
   ```text
   global_readout_with_good_init {'local': 0.8742, 'global': 0.0303}
   shot_noise_at_prediction      {'exact': 0.9, '100_shots': 0.85}
   ```
   **What it means:** a global observable (parity of all 10 qubits) stays flat even with a good start: the readout choice matters as much as init. Predicting from 100 shots flips one borderline point.

## Failure lab: a bigger model that cannot learn

1. **Inject:** scale to 10 qubits × 20 layers with the textbook init, angles uniform in `[0, 2π)`.
2. **Symptom:** median `|grad|` 0.0208, below the 1000-shot floor 0.0224, for 55% of inits (step 1).
3. **Diagnose:** the floor is the standard deviation of one parameter-shift estimate (`√2/2/√1000`). A gradient below it cannot be distinguished from noise, so updates are random. The 6 × 12 line (0.1121) shows the trend before it bites.
4. **Fix:** `solution_init.py` draws angles from `N(0, 0.05²)`, so every layer starts near the identity, and keeps the local `⟨Z0⟩` readout.
   ```text
   {"n": 10, "layers": 20, "readout": "local", "median_abs_grad": 0.863, "below_shot_floor": 0.0}
   {"model": "quantum 2q x 2 layers, near-identity init", "train_acc": 0.95, "test_acc": 0.9}
   ```
5. **Proof:**
   ```text
   $ BUILD_TASK_MODULE=classifier_lab python -m unittest test_build_task
   AssertionError: 0.0163 not greater than np.float64(0.224)
   FAILED (failures=1)
   $ python -m unittest test_build_task
   Ran 2 tests in 3.101s
   OK
   ```
   The bar is 10× the shot floor (0.224), and the second test checks the small model still classifies.

## Common confusions

- **Q:** 0.9 vs 0.85 shows quantum advantage. — **A:** One test point, and the 5-seed line is a tie; lesson 18 formalises this.
- **Q:** A small gradient at epoch 59 means a barren plateau. — **A:** It converged; the plateau is small gradients at *initialisation* (`gradient_scan`).
- **Q:** Good init fixes everything. — **A:** `global` 0.0303 with the good init: global readouts stay flat.
