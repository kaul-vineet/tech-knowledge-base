# The QFT as a building block — explained

## The intuition

The quantum Fourier transform (QFT) is the discrete Fourier transform you already know from signal processing, applied to the `2^n` amplitudes of a state. It turns a basis state `|x⟩` into an equal-magnitude superposition whose phases rotate at speed `x`: entry `k` gets phase `x·k/N` turns. Run backwards, it turns such a "rotating phase pattern" back into the number `x`.

That reverse direction is the useful one. Algorithms such as phase estimation and Shor's period finding first encode an unknown frequency or period into phases, then apply the inverse QFT to read it out as bits. The QFT itself never "computes the answer"; it converts between "number in bits" and "number in phases".

The circuit is cheap (one `H` per qubit, controlled phases `π/2, π/4, …`, then swaps: about `n²/2` gates), but you read one measured sample, not all `N` outputs.

**Analogy:** a phone's spectrum analyser: a pure tone in, one peak out.
Where the analogy breaks: the analyser shows you the whole spectrum; a measurement after the QFT shows you one peak, sampled at random.

```text
|5> = q2 q1 q0 = 1 0 1
  --QFT--> amplitudes all 0.3536, phases 5k/8 turns: 0, 0.625, 0.25, 0.875, ...
  --QFT^-1--> |5> again
period r input --QFT--> peaks at multiples of N/r   (period 2, N=8: '000', '100')
```

The one thing to remember: the QFT converts a period hidden in phases into a number you can measure, and the inverse does the reverse.

## Walkthrough: what actually happens when you run it

1. **You run** `python crosscheck_numpy.py`.
   **What happens inside:** numpy multiplies the gate matrices of `qft_circuit(3)` and compares the product with `np.fft.ifft(eye(8), norm="ortho")`, which has the QFT's `+i` sign and `1/√N` factor; it also checks the inverse and prints `QFT|5⟩`.
   **What you see:**
   ```text
   QFT gates vs numpy DFT, max |difference|: 0.0
   inverse QFT gates vs numpy DFT^-1 (np.fft.fft ortho): 0.0
   QFT|5> magnitudes: [0.3536, 0.3536, 0.3536, 0.3536, 0.3536, 0.3536, 0.3536, 0.3536]
   QFT|5> phases (turns): [0.0, 0.625, 0.25, 0.875, 0.5, 0.125, 0.75, 0.375]
   ```
   **What it means:** magnitudes are all `1/√8 = 0.3536`, so measuring `QFT|5⟩` directly is uniform noise; the information is entirely in the phases, `5k/8 mod 1`. Note naming: numpy's `ifft` is the QFT, `fft` is its inverse.

2. **You run** `python qft_lab.py`.
   Expected (not run here): `hand-built QFT == numpy DFT: True`, `== QFTGate: True`, `QFT then inverse QFT == identity: True`, the same phases as step 1, then the circuit drawing.

3. **You run** `python try_it.py`.
   **What you see:**
   ```text
   no_swaps        numpy {'max_error_vs_dft': 0.7071, 'after_bit_reversing_rows': 0.0}
   negated_angles  numpy {'max_error_vs_dft': 0.7071, 'max_error_vs_np_fft_fft': 0.0}
   ```
   **What it means:** dropping the swaps does not break the maths, it permutes the outputs (bit-reversed rows, error 0.0 after reordering). Negating the angles gives the other transform, a classic sign-convention bug.

4. **You run** `python solution_period.py` (build task: read a period).
   **What you see:**
   ```text
   period 2 offset 1: QFT probabilities {'000': 0.5, '100': 0.5} -> period 2
   period 4 offset 3: QFT probabilities {'000': 0.25, '010': 0.25, '100': 0.25, '110': 0.25} -> period 4
   ```
   **What it means:** a state spread evenly over `offset, offset+r, …` gives QFT peaks at multiples of `N/r` (8/2 = 4 → `'100'`). The offset only changes phases, not where the peaks are, which is why period finding works whatever the starting point.

## Common confusions

- **Q:** The QFT is an exponentially faster FFT. — **A:** Only the gate count is small; you read one sample, not 8 outputs. Magnitudes `0.3536` everywhere show measuring right after it tells you nothing.
- **Q:** No swaps means a wrong circuit. — **A:** `after_bit_reversing_rows: 0.0`: it is a relabelling.
- **Q:** `fft` and the QFT are the same. — **A:** The QFT matches `ifft` (positive sign); `negated_angles` matches `fft`.
