# Benchmarking honestly against classical solvers — explained

## The intuition

"Our quantum solver beat the classical one" is easy to fake by accident: give one solver more attempts, report its best run, or test on the one instance where it shines. The lab ships such a benchmark, watches it crown QAOA, then rebuilds it fairly.

A fair benchmark fixes the rules first: same *sample budget* (200 cut evaluations), same 10 seeds, same instances, medians and success rates over all runs. Code enforces the budget (`BudgetViolation`) and picks the winner (median ratio, then success, then samples to best; ties go to the classical baseline). An exact solver supplies the true optimum.

Two metrics matter. The **approximation ratio** is best cut / optimum. **TTS99** (time to solution at 99%) asks how many samples you need, repeating runs, to see the optimum with 99% confidence: `budget × ln(0.01) / ln(1 − success)`.

**Analogy:** an A/B test where one arm got four times the traffic and you reported its best day.
Where the analogy breaks: here you can also compute the exact right answer (brute force) for small sizes, so you can grade both arms absolutely.

```text
instances (3-regular, n = 8, 12, 16) x seeds 0..9
   |-- exact (brute force)  -> optimum
   |-- greedy   \
   |-- annealing > each: budget 200 samples, used <= budget (else BudgetViolation)
   |-- qaoa p=1 /
summarise: median_ratio, success, median_first_best, tts99 -> winner rule (ties -> classical)
```

The one thing to remember: a benchmark is only honest if the rules (budget, seeds, statistic, winner rule) are fixed in code before you look at the numbers.

## Walkthrough: what actually happens when you run it

1. **You run** `python bench_lab.py` (the shipped, biased harness).
   **What happens inside:** `compare()` gives QAOA 4× the budget and takes `max()` over 10 seeds; greedy and annealing run once (`seeds[:1]`) at a quarter budget; every row still prints `"budget": 200`.
   **What you see:**
   ```text
   {"n": 12, "solver": "greedy", "budget": 200, "runs": 1, "median_ratio": 0.8, "success": 0.0, …}
   {"n": 12, "solver": "annealing", "budget": 200, "runs": 1, "median_ratio": 0.8667, "success": 0.0, …}
   {"n": 12, "solver": "qaoa", "budget": 200, "runs": 1, "median_ratio": 1.0, "success": 1.0, "median_first_best": 426, …}
   {"n": 12, "winner": "qaoa", "method": "naive (unequal budget, best seed)"}
   ```
   **What it means:** `median_first_best: 426` is the giveaway: QAOA found its best cut after 426 samples while claiming a 200 budget.

## Failure lab: a benchmark that manufactures a win

1. **Inject:** the shipped `compare()` (BOOST, `seeds[:1]`, `max(results)`).
2. **Symptom:** QAOA "wins" n = 12 with 1.0 vs 0.8 / 0.8667 while every row claims budget 200.
3. **Diagnose:** the build-task tests probe the harness, not the solvers:
   ```text
   AssertionError: RuntimeError not raised
   AssertionError: Lists differ: [1, 1] != [4, 4]
   AssertionError: Lists differ: [(25, 1)] != [(100, 1), (100, 2), (100, 3)]
   FAILED (failures=4)
   ```
   No over-budget check, one run instead of all seeds, and unequal budgets.
4. **Fix:** `solution_fair.py`: same budget and seeds, medians, `BudgetViolation`.
5. **Proof:** `python -m unittest test_build_task test_fitness` → `Ran 6 tests in 4.665s` … `OK`.

## Walkthrough: the decision

1. **You run** `python solution_fair.py`.
   **What you see:**
   ```text
   {"n": 8, "winner": "annealing", "method": "fair (same budget, same 10 seeds, medians)"}
   {"n": 12, "solver": "greedy", "budget": 200, "runs": 10, "median_ratio": 0.9333, "success": 0.4, … "tts99_samples": 1803, …}
   {"n": 12, "solver": "qaoa", "budget": 200, "runs": 10, "median_ratio": 0.9667, "success": 0.5, … "tts99_samples": 1329, …}
   {"n": 16, "solver": "greedy", "budget": 200, "runs": 10, "median_ratio": 1.0, "success": 0.9, … "tts99_samples": 400, …}
   {"n": 16, "solver": "qaoa", "budget": 200, "runs": 10, "median_ratio": 1.0, "success": 0.6, … "tts99_samples": 1005, "local_s": 0.78}
   {"n": 16, "winner": "greedy", "method": "fair (same budget, same 10 seeds, medians)"}
   ```
   **What it means:** at n = 8 everything finds the optimum (the tie-break decides). QAOA's n = 12 lead is 5/10 vs 4/10 successes on one graph, which ADR 0001 itself calls not significant with 10 seeds. At n = 16 greedy is clearly better. The lesson text quotes 1.69 s of simulator time for QAOA at n = 16; this machine printed 0.78 s. Local seconds are machine-dependent, which is why the harness never judges them.

2. **You run** `python try_it.py`.
   **What you see:**
   ```text
   other_instances {1: 'annealing', 2: 'greedy', 3: 'qaoa', 4: 'annealing', 5: 'annealing'}
   exact_in_budget ({'greedy': 1.0, 'annealing': 1.0, 'qaoa': 1.0, 'exact': 1.0}, 'greedy')
   ```
   **What it means:** QAOA wins 1 of 5 other 12-node graphs: the n = 12 "win" was instance luck. With 4096 samples, exact enumeration fits in the budget and *proves* optimality.

3. **The ADR** (`docs/adr/0001-fair-benchmark.md`) decides: all comparisons go through the fair harness; for n ≤ 20 the service solves exactly; QAOA stays a research option until it wins on more than single instances, under noise, and on wall time including queueing (lesson 17).

4. **The fitness function** `test_fitness.py` checks the *method*: equal budgets, 10 runs per solver, and a recomputed report identical to the stored one (excluding `local_s`). The shipped harness fails `test_reference_config`.

## Common confusions

- **Q:** QAOA won n = 12, so it is better at that size. — **A:** One graph, 0.5 vs 0.4 success; `other_instances` shows 1 of 5.
- **Q:** Faster local seconds mean a faster solver. — **A:** Simulator time says nothing about hardware; the harness compares samples.
- **Q:** Brute force is not a real competitor. — **A:** At n ≤ 20 it is the best option: exact and provably optimal.
