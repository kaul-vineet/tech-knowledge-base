# Reading seminar 27: Grover (1996) and Preskill (2018) — explained

## The big picture

Two short papers bracket what this course has built. Grover's 1996 paper introduced the search algorithm you ran in "Small Grover search" and derived in "Foundations III: algorithms and complexity" (T25.1). Preskill's 2018 paper named the *NISQ* era (noisy intermediate-scale quantum), the regime simulated in "Noise models: how Bell and GHZ results degrade" and "A hybrid variational loop (VQE) under shot noise". Reading them after the labs is deliberate: you can now check their claims with code instead of taking them on trust.

This guide tells you what to read first, what you can skip, the three ideas to take away from each, and how each idea connects to a lab. It summarises; read the papers themselves (both are free: Grover on arXiv, Preskill in *Quantum* under CC BY 4.0) and quote only short, attributed phrases.

## Reading guide: Grover, "A fast quantum mechanical algorithm for database search" (1996)

**Read first:** the abstract and the short statement of the problem. *Why:* the "unsorted phone directory" framing is the claim everyone quotes, and you should know precisely what is being compared: about `N/2` classical look-ups for a 50% chance versus `O(√N)` quantum steps.

**Then read:** the description of one iteration and the "inversion about average" argument. *Why:* it is the heart of the paper and maps one-to-one onto your lab's diffusion step.

**Skim or skip on a first pass:** the general background on quantum computation and the decomposition into elementary operations. You already know the circuit model from L100–L200, and the implementation details are written in the notation of the time.

**Three ideas to take away:**
1. *Inversion about average is a reflection.* The update `a_i → 2·mean(a) − a_i` is the matrix `2|s⟩⟨s| − I` (problem P2). Combined with the oracle's sign flip, two reflections make a rotation, which is exactly the T25.1 proof.
2. *The count grows like √N, and it must be computed.* Grover's iteration count corresponds to `k = ⌊π/(4θ)⌋`. The lab's `overshoot` result (0.3301 at k = 3 for 8 items) is the practical consequence: stopping late destroys the answer.
3. *Near-optimality was claimed, then proved by others.* The paper asserts it is close to the best possible; the matching lower bound came from BBBV (1997), and Boyer et al. (1998) gave the exact formula and handled unknown `M`.

**How it connects to the labs:** run the check and compare with the paper's scaling claim.
```text
  N =  1024, M = 1: best k =  25 (floor(pi/(4 theta)) =  25, (pi/4) sqrt(N/M) =   25.13), P = 0.9995
  overshoot, N = 64: P at k = 6 is 0.9966, at k = 12 it is 0.0001 (rotation keeps turning)
```
For `N = 10^6` (problem P1) the same formula gives 785 iterations versus 500,000 classical look-ups; `research/l500_checks.py` recomputes it (`40 problems, 26 recomputed (64 values)` … `OK`).

**Read critically:** "database search" suggests speeding up SQL. In practice the "database" is an oracle circuit that must compute the predicate *reversibly*, on every branch at once. Lesson 18's fairness rules apply: compare against the best classical method on the same budget, and include error-correction overheads from lesson 19 before talking about wall-clock time.

## Reading guide: Preskill, "Quantum Computing in the NISQ era and beyond" (2018)

**Read first:** the abstract and the introduction, where the term NISQ is defined. *Why:* "intermediate-scale" (the abstract speaks of 50-100 qubits) and "noisy" (no error correction) are two separate limits, and the paper's argument depends on keeping them apart.

**Then read:** the discussion of what NISQ devices might be good for: simulating quantum systems, variational and optimisation methods (QAOA, VQE), and quantum machine learning, each with the caveats Preskill attaches. *Why:* these are your L300 labs, and his caveats are the ones the labs measured.

**Skim or skip on a first pass:** the more physics-oriented passages on many-body simulation and annealing hardware, unless that is your domain. Come back to them after the essay question.

**Three ideas to take away:**
1. *Noise caps circuit size.* With error rate ε per gate, reliable circuits have roughly `1/ε` gates. Problem P3's simple model: `(1 − 0.001)^1000 = 0.3677` but `(1 − 0.001)^100 = 0.9048`. Your lab saw the same shape: GHZ-4 success fell from 0.8622 to 0.7073 as two-qubit noise grew.
2. *A speed-up is relative to the best classical method.* Preskill defines speed-up against the best classical hardware and algorithm, and notes that a quantum-inspired classical algorithm removed a claimed exponential speed-up for recommendation systems. "Benchmarking honestly against classical solvers" builds that rule into code.
3. *Fault tolerance remains the goal.* NISQ devices are a step, not the destination. "Resource estimation: logical vs physical qubits and feasibility" shows why: useful algorithms like Shor's need error-corrected logical qubits at large physical overhead.

**How it connects to the labs:** the VQE lab's failure (error 1.5645 under shot noise, 0.0064 with SPSA) and the classifier's flat gradients at 10 qubits × 20 layers are concrete instances of his caveats about variational methods.

**Read critically:** test one prediction against evidence available since 2018. The 2019 "supremacy" claim and its later classical rebuttals (lesson 26, problem P6: a 5.8-million-fold shrink in the classical estimate) show both that his "beyond classical on some tasks" prediction arrived and that the target keeps moving.

## Worked problems

**P2.** Show inversion about average equals `2|s⟩⟨s| − I`.
1. Define `|s⟩ = (1/√N) Σ_i |i⟩`. *Why:* the uniform state is the "average" direction.
2. Compute `(|s⟩⟨s| a)_i = (1/N) Σ_j a_j = mean(a)`. *Why:* `⟨s|a⟩ = (1/√N)Σ a_j`, times the `i`-th entry of `|s⟩`, `1/√N`.
3. So `((2|s⟩⟨s| − I)a)_i = 2·mean(a) − a_i`. *Why:* linearity. This is the line `psi = 2 * psi.mean() - psi` in the T25.1 check.

**P3.** NISQ noise model, success `(1 − ε)^G`.
1. `ε = 10^-3`, `G = 1000`: `0.3677`. *Why:* each gate must succeed independently.
2. `G = 100`: `0.9048`; `ε = 10^-4`, `G = 1000`: also `0.9048`. *Why:* only the product `εG` matters when ε is small, since `(1 − ε)^G ≈ e^{−εG}`.
3. Success stays near `1/e` when `G ≈ 1/ε`. *Why:* this is the quantitative core of "noise limits reliable circuit size".

## Common confusions

- **Q:** Grover speeds up databases. — **A:** It speeds up *oracle* queries; building the oracle can dominate the cost.
- **Q:** NISQ means "useless". — **A:** Preskill argues it is useful for exploration and some tasks, with caveats; the labs measured those caveats.
- **Q:** Citing a paper means copying its text into notes. — **A:** Summarise in your own words; Grover's arXiv licence covers arXiv, not you; Preskill's CC BY 4.0 requires attribution (P6).
