# Ethics: reliability as a professional duty - outages, blameless post-mortems, data residency and energy — explained

## The big picture
The technical lessons treated failures as puzzles. This lesson treats them as harms to people who never chose your architecture. The case — AWS's own summary of the 7 December 2021 us-east-1 event — is made of mechanisms you have already built: a retry storm whose back-off failed ("Retries with exponential backoff, jitter and a retry budget"), overload that queues instead of shedding ("Backpressure and load shedding: bounded queues, 503 + Retry-After, AIMD concurrency limits"), status and support tooling inside the failure domain, and a board asking for cross-region failover ("Multi-region design: latency budget, leader placement, follower reads, failover RTO/RPO") to be shipped in a week, tested with "Chaos engineering: hypotheses, steady state, blast radius and seeded fault campaigns". The ethical question is not "was someone careless?" but "what do professionals owe the people downstream, and how do we decide under pressure?" Below, one framework is applied step by step, then a contrasting view tests it.

## Worked analysis: the ACM Code of Ethics (2018), step by step
The case extension: you lead platform engineering for an EU telehealth booking service hosted only in the affected region. After the outage, the board wants automatic failover within a week; the cheapest region with capacity is outside the EEA.

**Step 1 — Name the stakeholders, including those who cannot opt out.** *Why this step:* ACM 1.2 (avoid harm) is about people affected, not only customers. Patients who cannot book or reschedule; clinic and call-centre staff whose phone and chat tools failed; your on-call engineers working with impaired monitoring; data subjects whose health records would be copied abroad; the supervisory authority; people affected by the energy use of a permanent standby.

**Step 2 — Separate harms from the design choices that amplified them.** *Why:* blameless analysis (Google SRE ch. 15) looks for fixable systems, not culprits — "you can't fix people, but you can fix systems and processes". Three amplifiers in AWS's summary are design decisions: client back-off with a latent defect, a status dashboard whose failover depended on the failing region, and support tooling on the impaired network. A blameless sentence reads: "an automated scaling activity triggered a client behaviour not seen in production; back-off did not engage because of a latent issue" — not "the scaling team broke the network".

**Step 3 — Map facts to specific principles.** *Why:* vague appeals to "doing the right thing" cannot be argued with. ACM 3.7 (special care for infrastructure) applies because a region's control plane serves thousands of organisations. ACM 2.5 (comprehensive evaluation of risks) applies to the one-week failover. ACM 1.6 (privacy) and GDPR Art. 32 and Chapter V apply to replicating health data. ACM 1.1 (environmental sustainability) applies to running a second region. IEEE item 1 adds prompt disclosure of factors endangering the public.

**Step 4 — Generate options and quantify them.** *Why:* principles conflict (availability vs privacy vs energy), so numbers make the trade-off visible. A 99.95% monthly target allows 21.6 minutes; the 2021 event's 412 minutes was 7.8 times a whole year's 99.99% budget. Lab 19 measured async failover losing 16 acknowledged writes, majority-sync 0. At 40 kW and PUE 1.4 one site uses 490.6 MWh a year; a hot standby doubles it to 981.1 MWh. Options: (a) non-EEA hot standby in a week; (b) second EEA region, warm standby, after a game day; (c) static "call this number" page in any region, no personal data.

**Step 5 — Evaluate each option against the principles.** *Why:* this is where 2.5 bites. Option (a) ships untested failover (a classic outage cause), transfers health data under Chapter V — needing an adequacy decision or appropriate safeguards with enforceable rights, a basis Schrems II showed can disappear — and doubles energy. Option (b) avoids the transfer, can be measured before promising an RTO/RPO, and costs a fraction of hot standby. Option (c) is cheap, privacy-neutral and helps immediately.

**Step 6 — Decide, communicate limits, and know when to escalate.** *Why:* ACM 1.3 asks for honesty about limitations; ACM 2.1/2.3 say professionals should resist inducements to neglect quality and challenge rules that cause recognisable harm. Recommend (c) now and (b) with a dated plan, state plainly what is not yet protected, and treat the one-week deadline as negotiable. If leaders insist on a path you believe will cause significant harm, ACM 1.2 allows that it "may be necessary to blow the whistle" after careful assessment — first through internal channels, with evidence (measured RPO, transfer analysis). This is policy-level reasoning, not legal advice; the DPO must review.

## A contrasting view: cost-benefit (consequentialist) analysis
A consequentialist asks only which option produces the best total outcome. On that view the board's request may be right: an outage harms thousands of patients now, while the transfer risk is probabilistic and the energy cost is diffuse. If option (a) prevents, say, several hours of missed bookings per year, its benefit might outweigh a small privacy risk and 490.6 MWh. The ACM analysis answers in two ways. First, duties are not fully tradeable: GDPR sets a legal floor and ACM 1.6 is not waived by a good average. Second, the cost-benefit sum is only as good as its inputs — and an untested one-week failover has an unknown benefit (it may fail when needed) and a known risk (lost writes, legal basis that can vanish). Both views agree on one thing: measure. A game day turns the debate from opinion into numbers.

## Worked problems
**26-P1.** 99.95% per 30-day month: allowed downtime?
1. Unavailability = 1 − 0.9995 = 0.0005. *Why:* the budget is what the promise leaves.
2. Minutes per month = 30 × 24 × 60 = 43,200.
3. 0.0005 × 43,200 = 21.6 minutes. *Why it matters:* one half-hour failover breaks the promise.

**26-P5.** 40 kW IT load, PUE 1.4, all year; then with a same-size hot standby?
1. Facility power = 40 × 1.4 = 56 kW. *Why:* PUE scales IT load to facility load.
2. × 8,760 h = 490,560 kWh ≈ 490.6 MWh.
3. Hot standby doubles it: 981.1 MWh. *Why it matters:* warm standby or graceful degradation may meet the need at a fraction.

## Common confusions
**Q:** Blameless means nobody is accountable. — **A:** Accountability moves to owned action items, reviewed and shared.

**Q:** Encrypting the replica removes the transfer question. — **A:** It reduces risk but, at policy level, the data still leaves the EEA; keys held in the EU help, they do not settle it.

**Q:** Reliability ethics only concerns uptime. — **A:** Privacy (ACM 1.6) and energy (ACM 1.1) are part of the same decision.
