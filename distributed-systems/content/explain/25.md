# Foundations III: performance theory - M/M/1, Little's law, tail amplification, Amdahl and USL — explained

## The big picture
Several labs produced numbers that felt surprising: in "Backpressure and load shedding: bounded queues, 503 + Retry-After, AIMD concurrency limits" mean wait grew fivefold between 80% and 95% load, and an unbounded queue reached a p99 of 4.6 s; in "Timeouts: fail fast instead of hanging" a p99.9 timeout still cut some healthy calls; "Distributed tracing with W3C traceparent: propagation, spans as JSON lines, critical path" showed one slow leaf owning the critical path; "Multi-region design: latency budget, leader placement, follower reads, failover RTO/RPO" summed latency terms into a budget. Four results explain them: queueing delay explodes as utilisation nears 1; occupancy = rate × time, always; fan-out multiplies tail probability; and parallelism has a ceiling — or even a peak. Checks: `labs/L500/25-performance-theory/perf_proofs.py`.

### T25.1 The M/M/1 queue
**In plain words** One server, random arrivals and service times. At utilisation ρ the mean time in system is (service time) × 1/(1 − ρ): 2× at 50%, 5× at 80%, 10× at 90%.

**Why it is true (intuition)** The queue length is a random walk pushed up at rate λ and down at rate μ; near ρ = 1 the downward push barely wins, so long excursions become common.
1. States n = 0, 1, 2…; up at λ, down at μ.
2. In steady state flow across each cut balances: λ p_n = μ p_{n+1}, so p_n = ρ^n p_0.
3. Probabilities sum to 1 ⇒ p_0 = 1 − ρ (the idle fraction).
4. Mean L = ρ/(1 − ρ); Little's law gives W = 1/(μ − λ).

**See it in numbers** `python perf_proofs.py mm1`
```
  rho=0.5: W sim  2.001 theory  2.000 | L sim  1.000 theory  1.000 | P(N=0) sim 0.501 theory 0.500 | P(N=2) sim 0.124 theory 0.125
  rho=0.9: W sim  9.838 theory 10.000 | L sim  8.846 theory  9.000 | P(N=0) sim 0.103 theory 0.100 | P(N=2) sim 0.083 theory 0.081
```
Simulation matches the formulas; at 0.9 convergence is slower (9.838 vs 10), typical near saturation.

**Where it shows up in practice** Lesson 16's sweep: the last 15% of load multiplied the wait — why it sheds before the queue grows.

### T25.2 Little's law (Little 1961; Little & Graves 2008)
**In plain words** Average number inside = arrival rate × average time inside. No assumption about distributions, servers or order.

**Why it is true (intuition)** Draw n(t) over time. The area under it can be counted two ways: instant by instant, or customer by customer (each contributes its stay).
1. Window starts and ends empty ⇒ area = sum of all stays.
2. Divide by T: left side is L; right side is (arrivals/T) × (mean stay) = λW.
3. Other windows differ only by edge terms that vanish relative to T.

**See it in numbers** `python perf_proofs.py little`
```
    area under n(t) = 163,913.199728; sum of sojourn times = 163,913.199728; relative difference 1.1e-15
    L = 1.311536; lambda * W = 0.800228 * 1.638952 = 1.311536
```
Non-Markov arrivals and log-normal service, and the identity is exact to rounding error.

**Where it shows up in practice** Lesson 16's bound: 40 queued ÷ 400/s = 100 ms of waiting — Little's law used as a sizing tool.

### T25.3 Tail amplification under fan-out
**In plain words** If a request waits for n leaves and each is slow with probability p, the request is slow with probability 1 − (1 − p)^n ≈ np.

**Why it is true (intuition)** The root is fast only if *every* leaf is fast.
1. Independent leaves: P(all fast) = (1 − p)^n.
2. For small p, (1 − p)^n ≈ 1 − np.
3. Invert: to keep the root slow-rate ≤ q, need p ≤ 1 − (1 − q)^(1/n) ≈ q/n.
4. With dependence, P(root slow) lies between max p_i and Σ p_i.

**See it in numbers** `python perf_proofs.py tail`
```
  fan-out 100: P(root waits for a slow leaf) sim 0.6329, 1 - 0.99^n = 0.6340
  to keep the root's p99 at the leaf-p99 level with fan-out 100, each leaf must meet its 0.999900 quantile (about p99.99)
```
A leaf's p99 becomes the root's *median* experience at fan-out 100.

**Where it shows up in practice** Lesson 3's p99.9 timeout: with fan-out, a leaf's 0.1% false-timeout rate multiplies at the root.

### T25.4 Amdahl's bound and the USL peak
**In plain words** A serial fraction σ caps speedup at 1/σ. Add a coherency cost κ (pairwise coordination) and throughput peaks at p* = √((1 − σ)/κ), then *falls*.

**Why it is true (intuition)** Serial work does not shrink; coordination grows with the number of pairs, p(p − 1).
1. Amdahl: time = σ + (1 − σ)/p ⇒ S(p) → 1/σ.
2. USL: C(p) = p / (1 + σ(p − 1) + κp(p − 1)).
3. Derivative's sign is that of 1 − σ − κp²: positive below p*, negative above.

**See it in numbers** `python perf_proofs.py usl`
```
  Amdahl sigma=0.05: S(2)=1.90, S(8)=5.93, S(16)=9.14, S(64)=15.42, S(1024)=19.64; bound 1/sigma = 20
  simulated closed system (p workers, think Exp(1), shared FIFO lock held 0.05 + 0.004(p-1)): C(1)=1.00, C(4)=3.99, C(8)=7.49, C(12)=9.97, C(16)=9.54, C(24)=7.40, C(32)=6.04, C(40)=5.10
  USL fit (sigma >= 0): sigma=0.0000, kappa=0.00429 -> predicted peak p*=15.3; measured peak at p=13 (C=10.17); throughput falls after the peak: C(40)/C(peak)=0.50
```
1,024 cores give 19.64× at 5% serial. A lock whose hold time grows with p produces a real peak near 13 workers; at 40 throughput halves.

**Where it shows up in practice** "Multi-region design…": every write funnels through one leader — a σ term — and more replicas add coordination, a κ term.

## Worked problems
**25-P1.** M/M/1 with λ = 80/s, μ = 100/s: ρ, L, W, Wq?
1. ρ = 80/100 = 0.8. *Why:* utilisation is arrival over service rate.
2. L = 0.8/0.2 = 4. *Why:* T25.1.
3. W = 1/(100 − 80) s = 50 ms; Wq = 50 − 10 = 40 ms. *Why:* subtract one service time.
4. Check with Little: 80 × 0.05 = 4 = L.

**25-P3.** Each leaf slow 1%: root slow rate at fan-out 100? Fan-out 50 with leaves slow 0.1%?
1. 1 − 0.99^100 = 0.634. *Why:* all 100 must be fast.
2. 1 − 0.999^50 = 0.049.
3. Lesson: tightening the leaf tail 10× beats halving fan-out.

## Common confusions
**Q:** 90% utilisation is efficient. — **A:** W = 10 service times (sim 9.838).

**Q:** Little's law needs Poisson arrivals. — **A:** The check used jittered arrivals and log-normal service; still exact.

**Q:** More nodes always add throughput. — **A:** The simulated system fell to half its peak at 40 workers.
