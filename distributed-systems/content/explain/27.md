# Reading seminar: Lamport (1978) and Ongaro & Ousterhout (2014) — explained

## The big picture
Two labs in this course are small re-implementations of famous papers. "Ordering events with Lamport clocks" coded the counter rules from Lamport's 1978 paper; "Replicated log with Raft (simplified): terms, commit index, re-election, election restriction" coded a slice of Raft. Lessons 23 and 24 proved the results the papers rely on (T23.1 is Lamport's clock condition; T24.4 compares Raft's election restriction with Paxos). Reading the originals shows what the labs left out, why the authors made their choices, and — most useful for an architect — which statements are *proved*, which are *measured* and which are *asserted*. This guide tells you where to spend your attention. It summarises; read the papers themselves at the URLs in the lesson.

A good order: Lamport first (short, foundational), then Raft (longer, practical). For each paper make four passes — model, claims, argument, evidence — and keep a two-column list: "claim" and "proved / measured / asserted".

## Reading guide: Lamport (1978), "Time, Clocks, and the Ordering of Events in a Distributed System"
**Read first.** The introduction and the section defining the partial order: the key move is defining "happened before" only from events the system can observe — program order and message send/receive — never from a physical clock. Then the logical-clocks section: the clock condition, its two sub-conditions, and the two implementation rules. You implemented exactly these in `lamport.py`.

**Read carefully.** The total-ordering section and the mutual-exclusion algorithm. This is where the paper's real contribution sits: once every process can order all requests the same way, each process can run the same state machine on the same ordered input — the seed of state machine replication, and therefore of Raft. Reconstruct the small counterexample showing why a central scheduler granting requests in arrival order is wrong.

**Skim on first reading.** The physical-clocks section and its appendix proof. Read the "anomalous behaviour" example (an action outside the system — a phone call — creates an ordering the system cannot see) and the bound's shape; skip the derivation until you need it.

**Three ideas to take away.**
1. Causality is a partial order defined by communication; concurrency is the absence of a causal path, not "the same time".
2. Counters that jump past received stamps respect causality in one direction only (lab 6's L=8 tie; T23.1's converse).
3. A shared total order of inputs turns coordination into replaying a deterministic state machine — and the approach stops if one process stops, a failure problem the paper explicitly leaves open.

**Connection to the labs.** Lab 6 tryIt 1 removed `max()` and got 2 violations — the receive rule is the whole trick. The open failure problem is exactly what FLP (T24.2) sharpens and what Raft solves with timing assumptions.

## Reading guide: Ongaro & Ousterhout (2014), "In Search of an Understandable Consensus Algorithm"
**Read first.** The motivation and the one-page summary figure of the protocol's state and RPCs: it is the specification you would implement. Then the five safety properties and which mechanism maintains each.

**Read carefully.** The safety section: the election restriction and the Leader Completeness argument by contradiction. Find exactly where it uses majority intersection and where it uses "voters refuse less up-to-date candidates". Then the figure showing why a leader may not commit an entry from an earlier term just by counting replicas. Lab 12's failure lab is the first of these mistakes made concrete.

**Skim on first reading.** Cluster membership changes, log compaction and the client-interaction section — important in production, not needed for the core argument. Read the user study's design and numbers critically rather than in detail.

**Three ideas to take away.**
1. Understandability as a design goal: decompose (election, replication, safety) and shrink the state space (logs never have holes; only the leader appends).
2. Safety never depends on timing; availability does — the timing requirement relates broadcast time, election timeout and mean time between failures.
3. The election restriction replaces Paxos's "adopt the highest accepted value" with "only an up-to-date node can win" — same quorum-intersection logic (T24.4), different place.

**Connection to the labs.** Lab 12 lost committed entries in 11 of 20 seeds without the restriction and 0 with it; the split vote at t=40 is the timing side. Lab 21's model checker found a related bug — a forgotten vote — that the paper prevents by persisting state before replying.

**Critical lens.** "More understandable than Paxos" is an *empirical* claim supported by a study of 43 students; Raft's safety is a *proved* claim. Your response essay should keep the two apart.

## Worked problems
**27-P1.** Messages per request–use–release cycle in Lamport's mutual exclusion, N = 5?
1. Requests to every other process: N − 1. *Why:* rule 1 broadcasts the timestamped request.
2. Acknowledgements back: up to N − 1. *Why:* rule 2; an ack can be skipped if a later-stamped message was already sent.
3. Releases: N − 1. Total ≤ 3(N − 1) = 12 for N = 5.

**27-P4.** Skew bound ε ≈ d(2κτ + ξ) with d = 3, κ = 1e-6, τ = 1 s, ξ = 1 ms.
1. Drift term: 2 × 1e-6 × 1 s = 2 µs per hop. *Why:* two clocks drift apart in opposite directions.
2. Add unpredictable delay: 0.002 ms + 1 ms = 1.002 ms per hop.
3. × diameter 3 = 3.006 ms. *Why it matters:* the delay term dominates; better oscillators barely help — lab 6's tryIt 2 showed 15 ms skew already breaks order.

## Common confusions
**Q:** Lamport's paper is about physical clock sync. — **A:** Mostly about logical order; physical clocks enter only to fix anomalies from outside the system.

**Q:** Raft's user study proves Raft is correct. — **A:** It measures understanding; correctness rests on the safety proof.

**Q:** Raft needs synchronised clocks. — **A:** It needs timeouts to behave for liveness only; safety holds under any timing.
