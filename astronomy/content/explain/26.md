# Ethics and responsibility: dark and quiet skies, observatory sites and open-data credit — explained

## The big picture
Every earlier lab treated the sky, the site and the data as given. This lesson asks who else has a stake in them. Three of the course's labs become ethical decisions here: the broker of "Time-domain alerts: difference detection, alert schema and broker filters" (lesson 18) must decide what to do with satellite streaks; the release gate of "Open data and science software practice: VO standards, licences, citation and validation" (lesson 21) must keep Gaia's NonCommercial term; and the planning of "Observation planning: airmass, twilight, Moon distance and a scheduler under constraints" (lesson 12) depends on how dark twilight really is.

The case: you lead the software team of a time-domain survey. In one month (1) a regulator invites comments on a constellation of several thousand satellites at 550 km, (2) a colleague proposes silently discarding alerts within 30 arcsec of predicted satellite tracks, (3) a consortium draft calls consultation for a second site on an Indigenous community's protected landscape "completed" after two public meetings, and (4) you plan to release a catalogue cross-matched with Gaia DR3.

The method the lesson asks for: read primary documents, name stakeholders, separate law from recommendation, decide transparently — and compute the few numbers yourself.

From `python sky_numbers.py`. 6.31 is the flux ratio 10^(0.4 × 2): a magnitude-5 satellite against the magnitude-7 recommendation (lesson 4's Pogson ratio). 23.0 deg: a 550 km satellite overhead stays sunlit past the end of astronomical twilight (18 deg, lesson 12's dark limit); at 350 km, 18.6 deg. The licence lines are lesson 21's rule applied to Gaia.

## Worked analysis
Framework: the ACM Code of Ethics and Professional Conduct (2018), principles 1.1 (contribute to society and human well-being, acknowledging all people are stakeholders), 1.2 (avoid harm), 1.3 (be honest and trustworthy), 1.5 (respect the work required to produce new ideas — credit), and 2.5 (give comprehensive and thorough evaluations of systems, including risks). Apply it step by step.

**Step 1 — name the stakeholders (1.1).** Why first: harms and duties are invisible until you list who bears them. Here: your team; downstream alert consumers (follow-up observatories, near-Earth-object programmes, amateurs); satellite operators and their customers, including underserved communities gaining connectivity; regulators and COPUOS states; the IAU dark-and-quiet-sky centre and radio observatories; Indigenous community members and cultural practitioners; funders; Gaia DPAC and future catalogue users; and, as the IAU centre names it, the shared night sky and nocturnal wildlife.

**Step 2 — separate fact, law and recommendation (1.3).** Why: a comment letter that blurs them is dishonest. The COPUOS conference room paper documents streaks worst in twilight and at low elevation, false positives for time-domain surveys, and radio interference. The magnitude-7 limit and ephemeris-accuracy targets are recommendations to states; the paper describes some national provisions (for example France's magnitude-7 objective) and notes that in 2023 no country had binding brightness legislation. Quote the numbers you computed: 6.31× and 23.0 deg.

**Step 3 — evaluate the silent filter (1.2, 1.3, 2.5).** Why this order: harm first, then disclosure, then evidence. Public two-line elements are accurate only to a few arcminutes (CRP), far coarser than a 30-arcsec mask, so the filter both misses streaks and deletes real transients elsewhere — harm to consumers who rely on completeness (1.2). Doing it silently misstates the system (1.3). Doing it unmeasured fails 2.5. Alternative: a documented `satellite_proximity` flag in the alert schema, best available ephemerides, completeness and false-positive rates published with and without the flag on injected transients (the lesson 17–18 method), and consumers choose. Code classifies; documentation discloses.

**Step 4 — the site statement (1.1, 1.3).** Why primary sources: to describe the Maunakea Thirty Meter Telescope dispute without adopting either side's framing. The Hawai'i Supreme Court's 2018 opinion records practitioners' view of the mountain as sacred, to be honoured in its natural state; the appellees' position that telescope use is an allowed use with mitigation; the cumulative impact of earlier development; and it affirms the permit under a public-trust framework. Act 255 (2022) created a stewardship authority including Native Hawaiian practitioners. An honest "consultation completed" needs early engagement before site selection, representatives the community chooses, full information, a documented record of concerns and responses, a real possibility of "no", and continuing governance. Two meetings do not meet that bar; writing "completed" would breach 1.3. The lessons transfer without presuming the new community's views.

**Step 5 — the catalogue (1.5).** Gaia data are CC BY-NC 3.0 IGO, so the release must keep NC (CC BY-NC 4.0), with the Gaia acknowledgement and DR3 citation. A CC BY-SA input cannot be combined automatically: a person decides (`needs a person` above).

**Step 6 — recommend and set checks (2.5).** Comment letter with quantified impacts, labelled recommendations; flag-not-drop in the broker; pause the site statement and fund community-directed consultation with the option not to proceed; release under CC BY-NC 4.0. Review after one season: alert completeness, false-positive rate, consultation record, licence-check results.

## A contrasting view
A consequentialist (cost–benefit) reading weighs the same facts differently. Broadband for remote and underserved communities is a large, widely shared benefit; the survey's losses are concentrated in twilight programmes and could be reduced by mitigation rather than by opposing the licence. On this view, silent filtering might even be acceptable if it measurably improved overall alert quality — the objection becomes "measure it" rather than "never do it". The ACM analysis and this view converge on measurement and disclosure; they differ on whether a harm to a minority of users (lost transients, an unconsenting community) can be traded against aggregate benefit. The Code's 1.1 and 1.2, and the court's public-trust framing, resist that trade; the cost–benefit view permits it when compensated. Naming which framework you used is part of deciding transparently.

## Common confusions
- **Q:** "The magnitude-7 limit is law." — **A:** in the CRP it is a recommendation; the paper notes no binding brightness legislation in 2023.
- **Q:** "Lower orbits solve the problem." — **A:** 350 km satellites enter shadow at 18.6 deg, but twilight impacts remain.
- **Q:** "Gaia data are open, so CC BY 4.0 is fine." — **A:** `NOT OK (drops ['NC'])`.
