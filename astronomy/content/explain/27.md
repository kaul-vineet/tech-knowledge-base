# Reading seminar: Hubble (1929) and the Gaia DR3 summary — explained

## The big picture
The course has used two papers without reading them. Hubble (1929) is the origin of the K = 500 km/s/Mpc that "Foundations III: cosmology and Bayesian data analysis" (lesson 25) converted into a 1.96 Gyr Hubble time. The Gaia DR3 summary (Gaia Collaboration, Vallenari et al. 2023) is what you must cite for the parallaxes in "Foundations II: stars and radiation - luminosity, the HR diagram and the distance ladder" (lesson 24), the cone searches of "Big catalogues: sky indexing, cone-search performance and TAP/ADQL" (lesson 19) and the licence logic of "Open data and science software practice: VO standards, licences, citation and validation" (lesson 21).

Reading a paper is like reviewing a design document: find the claim, the evidence, the method and the stated limits, then check the arithmetic yourself. This page summarises; it does not reproduce either paper — read them via the links in the lesson. Hubble's paper is free to read (no open licence shown, so quote only briefly); the DR3 article is CC BY 4.0, while the Gaia data are CC BY-NC 3.0 IGO — two licences for two things.

**See it in numbers**
```
Hubble 1929: K = 500 km/s/Mpc -> 1/K = 1.96 Gyr
NGC 7619: v = +3910 km/s (solar-motion corrected) / K = 7.82 Mpc (paper: 7.8 x 10^6 pc)
NGC 7619: m = 11.8 at 7.8 Mpc -> M = -17.66 (paper: -17.65)
22 nebulae without individual distances: 745 km/s at 1.4 Mpc -> K = 532 (paper: 530)
modern H0 = 67.4: NGC 7619 at 58.0 Mpc; K/H0 = 7.42 (the 1929 distance scale was too short by this factor)
Gaia DR3: >33 million radial velocities / ~1500 million sources with class probabilities = 2.2%
Gaia DR3: 34 months of data = 2.83 yr
```
From `python reading_checks.py`. 1.96 Gyr is shorter than the Earth's radiometric age — a tension the later distance recalibration removed. 7.82 Mpc and −17.66 reproduce the paper's own numbers to rounding: the paper is internally consistent. 532 is the paper's "K of 530" cross-check. 7.42 is how much too short the 1929 distance scale was. 2.2 % of classified sources have radial velocities — a selection effect to record.

## Reading guide
### Hubble (1929), PNAS 15, 168–173
**Read first** The opening paragraphs (the problem: is there a velocity–distance relation among nebulae?), the table of nebulae with distances and velocities, and the figure plotting velocity against distance. Then the closing paragraph with its caveats.

**Skip on a first pass** The details of the solar-motion components. Know only why they are there: each measured velocity includes the Sun's own motion projected onto that line of sight, so Hubble solves for it jointly with K (problem 27.4).

**The three ideas to take away**
1. The method: equations of condition rK + X cos α cos δ + Y sin α cos δ + Z sin δ = v, solved by least squares — the same idea as a fit with nuisance parameters (lessons 15 and 25).
2. The evidence is thin and Hubble says so: 24 distances from Cepheids, novae, brightest stars and cluster luminosities, with the most distant (Virgo) objects carrying the largest lever arm. He gives two solutions (individual nebulae and nine groups) and adopts round values between them.
3. The claim is hedged: a linear relation as a first approximation over a restricted range of distance. That matches lesson 25's theorem: cz = H0 d is first-order.

**How it connects to the labs** `reading_checks.py` recomputes his numbers; lesson 25's conjugate posterior is how you would combine his two solutions today instead of picking a value in between. The factor 7.42 came from the distance calibration (Cepheid zero point, "brightest stars"), not from the linear form — the same shared-calibration error as lesson 24's theorem part (d).

### Gaia DR3 summary, A&A 674, A1 (2023)
**Read first** The abstract and the overview of what DR3 adds to EDR3, then the summary table of data products and their sizes.

**Skip on a first pass** The per-product validation sections: they point to the dedicated DR3 papers and the online documentation, which you read only for the products you use.

**The three ideas to take away**
1. Coverage: 34 months of observations; astrometry and photometry largely as in EDR3, plus new products — radial velocities for more than 33 million objects, about 220 million BP/RP spectra, variability for about 10 million sources, astrophysical parameters, Solar System objects.
2. Subsets have selection functions: radial velocities exist only for a small bright subset (the 2.2 % above), so 3-D velocity studies inherit that selection.
3. A release is documented for reuse: what is new, what is validated, where caveats live. Your pipeline should encode the caveats as checks (lesson 21's gate), and cite the paper plus any product papers.

**How it connects to the labs** Lesson 19's ADQL query targets `gaiadr3.gaia_source`; lesson 24's first rung is parallax; lesson 21's release keeps NC because the data are CC BY-NC 3.0 IGO.

## Worked problems
**Problem 27.2 — NGC 7619.** *Why divide:* the paper's distance for this nebula comes from the relation itself, so d = v/K = 3910/500 = 7.82 Mpc (paper: 7.8 × 10⁶ pc). *Why the modulus:* the absolute magnitude tests whether the paper's numbers agree with each other: M = m − 5 log10(d/10 pc) = 11.8 − 5 log10(7.8 × 10⁵) = −17.66, against the paper's −17.65. *Conclusion:* consistent to rounding.

**Problem 27.5 — two DR3 fractions.** *Why a ratio:* to see how representative the radial-velocity sample is: 33/1500 = 2.2 %. *Why convert units:* 34 months / 12 = 2.83 years, the baseline that limits parallax and proper-motion precision. *Conclusion:* any analysis needing full 3-D velocities is restricted to a bright subset; record that selection.

## Common confusions
- **Q:** "Hubble's K was wrong, so the linear law was wrong." — **A:** K/H0 = 7.42 came from the distance scale; the linear form survived.
- **Q:** "The DR3 article is CC BY 4.0, so the data are too." — **A:** the data are CC BY-NC 3.0 IGO.
- **Q:** "Quoting the abstract at length is fine since it is CC BY." — **A:** legally allowed with attribution, but summarise in your own words; long quotes add nothing and can drift from the version of record.
