# N-body simulation: leapfrog vs Euler and energy conservation — explained

## The intuition
An N-body code repeats one small step: compute every body's pull from the others, then update velocities and positions. The physics is exact; the error lives in how you step.

Euler updates position and velocity from the old state at once, which on an orbit always overshoots outward: energy rises every orbit and Earth spirals away. Leapfrog splits the step — half kick, drift, half kick. That ordering is time-reversible and symplectic: the energy error oscillates instead of accumulating.

A quieter problem: the Sun starts at rest while the planets move, so the whole system carries momentum and drifts. The build task moves to the centre-of-mass frame.

**Analogy:** walking a circle by "look, then step straight" always lands outside it; turning half before and half after each step keeps you on it.
Where the analogy breaks: leapfrog steps are not exact; it conserves a nearby energy, so errors stay bounded rather than vanishing.

```
initial_state() --to_com_frame()--> pos, vel
12 000 x: accelerations(pos) --> step_euler | step_leapfrog (kick-drift-kick)
energy() start vs end --> |dE/E| ; Earth-Sun distance ; COM shift
```

The one thing to remember: for long orbits use a structure-preserving integrator, and prove it with energy.

## Walkthrough: what actually happens when you run it
### Step 1 — two integrators, one force law
**You run** `python nbody.py`

**What happens inside** Sun, Earth (1 au) and Jupiter (5.2026 au) with G = 4π² in au, years and solar masses. `accelerations()` builds all pair vectors by broadcasting; 12 000 steps of 0.001 yr.

**What you see**
```
Euler    12 yr, dt=0.001 yr: |dE/E| = 1.24e-02, Earth-Sun distance 1.55347 au, centre of mass moved 0.0315 au
leapfrog 12 yr, dt=0.001 yr: |dE/E| = 1.22e-12, Earth-Sun distance 1.00000 au, centre of mass moved 0.0315 au
```

**What it means / what would look wrong** Ten orders of magnitude separate the energy errors; Euler pushed Earth to 1.55 au. A leapfrog error growing with run length would mean asymmetric kicks.

### Step 2 — the drifting system
**You run** the same command; read the last column.

**What happens inside** `run()` compares the mass-weighted mean position at start and end.

**What you see**
```
centre of mass moved 0.0315 au
```

**What it means / what would look wrong** Both integrators show it, because both conserve momentum exactly: the drift is a bad initial frame, not numerical error.

### Step 3 — the build task: barycentric frame
**You run** `BUILD_TARGET=mine python -m unittest test_build_task -v`, then `python solution/nbody.py`.

**What happens inside** The solution subtracts the mass-weighted mean position and velocity from every body.

**What you see**
```
AssertionError: np.float64(0.0026296084103068222) not less than 1e-15
FAILED (failures=3)
leapfrog 12 yr, dt=0.001 yr: |dE/E| = 1.26e-12, Earth-Sun distance 1.00000 au, centre of mass moved 0.0000 au
```

**What it means / what would look wrong** The shipped total momentum is 0.0026; afterwards the centre of mass stays put and the energy error barely changes. Suite: `Ran 10 tests ... OK (skipped=1)`; REBOUND runs in CI.

## Common confusions
- **Q:** "Leapfrog wins because it is higher order." — **A:** the key is symplecticity: Euler's 1.24e-02 grows every orbit; leapfrog's stays bounded.
- **Q:** "A smaller dt fixes Euler." — **A:** it slows the drift; energy still rises monotonically.
- **Q:** "The 0.0315 au drift is numerical error." — **A:** it is identical for both integrators and vanishes in the barycentric frame.
