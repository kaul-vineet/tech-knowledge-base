# Foundations I: classical mechanics, gravitation and spherical trigonometry — explained

## The big picture
Earlier labs used formulas as tools: `period_years()` returned a^1.5, the N-body code set G = 4π², `altaz()` used one line of trigonometry. Here they are derived, their assumptions named, and each checked by an independent route. It explains three labs: "Orbits: Kepler's laws, Kepler's equation and a planet's position" (lesson 5), "N-body simulation: leapfrog vs Euler and energy conservation" (lesson 11) and "Coordinates and the sky: RA/Dec to Alt/Az - is a star above the horizon now?" (lesson 2). Everything follows from F = GMm/r², two conserved quantities (energy and angular momentum), and one dot product on the unit sphere.

All numbers below come from `python mechanics.py` (labs/L500/23-foundations-mechanics).

### Kepler's third law (circular two-body orbits)
**In plain words** Period depends only on orbit size and total mass: T² = 4π²a³/(G(M + m)).

**Why it is true (intuition)** Two bodies act like one body orbiting their combined mass; on a circle gravity supplies exactly the centripetal pull.
1. Write Newton's law for each body and subtract: the separation r obeys r̈ = −G(M + m) r / r³.
2. That is a one-body problem with "mass" M + m.
3. On a circle the acceleration is ω²a; set ω²a = G(M + m)/a².
4. With ω = 2π/T, solve for T².

**See it in numbers**
```
Earth (a = 1 au, GM_sun): T = 365.2569 d
Jupiter's own mass shortens its period by a factor 0.999523 (= (1 + m/M)^-1/2)
leapfrog circular orbit, m/M = 0.0009546: T = 0.99952317 yr vs formula 0.99952304 yr (rel. diff 1.3e-07)
```
365.2569 d: the sidereal year from GM_sun alone. 0.999523: Jupiter's own-mass correction. The leapfrog integrator, which knows nothing of the formula, matches it to 1.3e-07.

**Where it shows up in practice** `period_years()` in lesson 5 (T = a^1.5 in years and au) and G = 4π² in lesson 11: in those units GM_sun = 4π².

### Vis-viva equation and escape speed
**In plain words** On any Kepler orbit, speed at distance r depends only on r and the orbit's size: v² = GM(2/r − 1/a). Escape needs exactly √2 times circular speed.

**Why it is true (intuition)** Two conserved quantities pin down the energy at the turning points; it is then the same everywhere.
1. Specific energy E = v²/2 − GM/r is conserved.
2. Angular momentum h = r_p v_p = r_a v_a at perihelion and aphelion.
3. Equate energies at both apsides and eliminate v_a: E = −GM/(r_p + r_a) = −GM/(2a).
4. Put E back into the energy equation at any r: v² = GM(2/r − 1/a).
5. Escape means E = 0, i.e. 1/a = 0, so v_esc² = 2GM/r.

**See it in numbers**
```
e = 0.6 orbit, 100001 samples: a measured = 1.000000 au, max |v^2 - GM(2/r - 1/a)| / v^2 = 3.0e-08
escape speed: Earth surface 11.18 km/s; from the Sun at 1 au 42.1 km/s
launch at 0.99 v_esc from 1 au: after 100 yr r = 37.8 au, specific energy -0.785
launch at 1.01 v_esc from 1 au: after 100 yr r = 170.3 au, specific energy +0.794
```
The formula holds to 3.0e-08 along an eccentric orbit. A small change in launch speed flips the energy's sign: bound (−0.785) versus gone (+0.794).

**Where it shows up in practice** Lesson 11: Euler added energy, so by vis-viva a grew — Earth drifted to 1.55 au.

### Virial theorem for a bound gravitating system
**In plain words** For any bound gravitating system, averaged over a long time, twice the kinetic energy equals minus the potential energy: 2⟨K⟩ + ⟨U⟩ = 0.

**Why it is true (intuition)** A quantity that stays bounded cannot grow forever, so its average rate of change is zero; for gravity that rate is exactly 2K + U.
1. Define G_v = Σ pᵢ·rᵢ.
2. Its time derivative is 2K + Σ Fᵢ·rᵢ.
3. For inverse-square pair forces, Σ Fᵢ·rᵢ = U exactly.
4. Average over time: the mean of dG_v/dt tends to zero, so 2⟨K⟩ + ⟨U⟩ = 0.

**See it in numbers**
```
instantaneous 2K/|U| over an e = 0.6 orbit ranges 0.400 .. 1.600
time-averaged 2<K>/|<U>| over 3 whole orbits = 1.000000; over the first quarter orbit = 1.249
```
Snapshots swing; whole-orbit averages give exactly 1; a quarter orbit does not. The theorem is about long averages.

**Where it shows up in practice** Cluster masses (problem 23.6) assume a relaxed system.

### Spherical cosine rule and the altitude formula of lesson 2
**In plain words** On a sphere, cos a = cos b cos c + sin b sin c cos A. Applied to the triangle pole–zenith–star it is exactly `sin h = sin δ sin φ + cos δ cos φ cos H` in `sky.py`.

**Why it is true (intuition)** Put one vertex at the pole; the arc between two unit vectors has cosine equal to their dot product.
1. A = (0, 0, 1); B and C sit at colatitudes c and b, longitudes A apart.
2. cos a = B·C, which expands to the rule.
3. Use sides 90 − φ (pole to zenith), 90 − δ (pole to star), 90 − h (zenith to star) and angle H at the pole.
4. cos(90 − x) = sin x turns the rule into the altitude formula.

**See it in numbers**
```
spherical cosine rule on 10000 random triangles: max error 1.9e-14
sin h = sin d sin phi + cos d cos phi cos H vs rotation matrix, 20000 points: max |d alt| = 3.0e-13 deg
lesson-2 sky.altaz vs rotation, 10 stars at Gurgaon 15:30 UTC: max |d alt| 7.1e-15 deg, max |d az| 2.8e-14 deg; Deneb alt 56.27 deg
```
Rounding-level agreement three ways, including lesson 2's code imported unchanged (Deneb 56.27 deg).

**Where it shows up in practice** Lesson 2's `altaz()` and lesson 12's twilight tests.

## Worked problems
**Problem 23.2 — geostationary radius.** *Why this step:* the satellite must keep pace with Earth's rotation against the stars, so use the sidereal day: 86400 × 24 / 24.065709824279 = 86164.0905 s. *Next:* Kepler III with m ≪ M: r = (GM (T/2π)²)^(1/3); with GM_Earth = 3.986004e14 that is 42164.2 km. *Why subtract:* the question asks for altitude above the equator: 42164.2 − 6378.1 = 35786.1 km.

**Problem 23.7 — culmination of Vega and Polaris at Gurgaon.** *Why H = 0:* culmination is the meridian crossing. Then sin h = cos(δ − φ), so h = 90 − |φ − δ|. *Vega:* 90 − (38.7837 − 28.46) = 79.68° (north of the zenith, since δ > φ). *Polaris upper:* 90 − (89.2641 − 28.46) = 29.20°. *Lower (H = 180):* sin h = −cos(δ + φ) gives 27.72°. Polaris stays within 0.74° of the latitude — which is why `sky.py` printed 28.93 in lesson 2.

## Common confusions
- **Q:** "Kepler's third law needs a circular orbit." — **A:** problem 23.1 shows e cancels: only the semi-major axis matters.
- **Q:** "The virial theorem holds at every moment." — **A:** the instantaneous ratio runs 0.400 .. 1.600.
- **Q:** "The altitude formula is an approximation." — **A:** it is exact geometry; lesson 2's approximations are J2000 positions and no refraction.
