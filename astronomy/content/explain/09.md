# Aperture photometry: background annulus and signal-to-noise — explained

## The intuition
To measure a star, add up the pixels inside a circle. The sum also contains sky glow, so estimate the sky per pixel from a ring farther out and subtract sky × area. What remains is the net flux.

Every number needs an error bar. The star's photons, the sky's photons and the detector's read noise all scatter; the CCD equation adds their variances to give a signal-to-noise ratio (SNR). Bright stars are limited by their own photons, faint ones by sky and read noise.

**Analogy:** weighing a parcel in a tray, then subtracting the tray's weight.
Where the analogy breaks: the "tray" here is itself noisy, so subtracting it adds noise — the `npix/ring.size` term.

```
image --aperture_weights(r=6)--> w --sum(w*img)--> aperture sum
image --ring 10..15 px--> median --> sky/px
net = sum - npix*sky ;  noise = sqrt(net + npix(1 + npix/nring)(sky + RN^2)) ; SNR = net/noise
net --calibrate()--> magnitude +/- 1.0857/SNR
```

The one thing to remember: net = aperture sum − sky × area, and its error comes from the CCD equation.

## Walkthrough: what actually happens when you run it
### Step 1 — the measurement table
**You run** `python photometry.py`

**What happens inside** `make_image(seed=9)` draws 101 × 101 pixels: sky 100 e⁻/px, five Gaussian stars (σ 1.5 px), Poisson noise plus 5 e⁻ read noise. `measure()` sub-samples aperture weights 5 × 5 per pixel, takes the annulus median and applies the CCD equation.

**What you see**
```
star  true(e-)  measured(e-)  ratio   sky/px  SNR
   0     20000       20006.2  1.000   100.21  102.6
   3      1000         827.5  0.827   101.15    6.0
   4       300         436.0  1.453    99.28    3.2
aperture r=6 px holds 0.99966 of a star's light (Gaussian PSF)
```

**What it means / what would look wrong** Sky is within about 1 e⁻ of 100. Star 3 is 17 % low, but SNR 6.0 means a 1σ error of about 17 %: noise, not a bug. A miss on star 0 would be a bug.

### Step 2 — the error budget
**You run** the same command; read the SNR column.

**What happens inside** For star 0, about 113 aperture pixels × (100 + 5²) contribute roughly 14 000 e⁻² of background variance next to 20 000 from the star. For star 4 the background dominates.

**What you see**
```
   1      8000        7774.1  0.972    99.99   48.4
   2      3000        3046.8  1.016    99.50   21.0
```

**What it means / what would look wrong** SNR falls faster than flux once sky dominates. Using read noise 5 instead of 25 per pixel inflates faint-star SNR.

### Step 3 — the build task: calibrated magnitudes
**You run** `BUILD_TARGET=mine python -m unittest test_build_task -v` (shipped `calibrate()` raises), then `python solution/photometry.py`.

**What happens inside** Instrumental magnitudes from net counts per second; the zero point from star 0 = 10.000; σ_m = 2.5/ln 10/SNR = 1.0857/SNR.

**What you see**
```
star 0: V = 10.000 +/- 0.011 (reference: star 0 = 10.000)
star 4: V = 14.154 +/- 0.338 (reference: star 0 = 10.000)
```

**What it means / what would look wrong** 1.0857/102.6 = 0.011. Star 4's 0.338 mag honestly says "barely detected". Suite: `Ran 11 tests ... OK (skipped=1)`.

## Common confusions
- **Q:** "Use the annulus mean." — **A:** the median resists a neighbour star in the ring.
- **Q:** "A bigger aperture is always better." — **A:** r = 6 already holds 0.99966 of the light; more radius adds only sky noise.
- **Q:** "Ratio 1.453 for star 4 is a bug." — **A:** at SNR 3.2 the error is ~31 %; this is 1.5σ.
