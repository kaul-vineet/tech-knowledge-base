# Reading seminar: Diffie & Hellman (1976) and Shannon (1949) — explained

## The big picture
These two papers frame everything in this topic. Shannon (1949) asked how secret a cipher can be against an enemy with *unlimited* computing power, and answered with probability: perfect secrecy is possible, but only with as much key as message. Diffie and Hellman (1976) accepted that bound and escaped it by changing the question to what an enemy can do with *limited* computation — which made public keys, key exchange and digital signatures possible. Lesson 25's one-time-pad theorem is Shannon's result; lesson 23's toy key exchange is Diffie and Hellman's; **Key exchange and hybrid encryption (X25519 + HKDF + AEAD)**, **Digital signatures with Ed25519** and **A local certificate authority** are their proposals in production form. Read Shannon first: Diffie and Hellman cite his one-time-pad result and define themselves against it. This guide tells you where to spend your time, what to skip, and what to take away; it summarises and never replaces the papers.

## Paper 1: Shannon, "Communication Theory of Secrecy Systems" (1949)
**Read first.** The short introduction and the opening of Part I, where Shannon models a secrecy system as a set of transformations chosen by a key with known probabilities, and states the assumption that the enemy knows the system. Then go to Part II and read the definition of perfect secrecy and the theorem that follows it, slowly; compare with lesson 25's T25.1 side by side. Finish Part II with equivocation and the unicity distance — the idea that ciphertext eventually determines the key because language is redundant.

**Skim or skip on a first pass.** The algebra of combining secrecy systems (products and weighted sums) in Part I, the long catalogue of classical ciphers, and the detailed equivocation curves for specific ciphers. They matter historically but not for the three ideas below. Part III on practical secrecy deserves a skim for two terms you will meet again — *work characteristic* and the design principles usually called *confusion and diffusion*.

**Three ideas to take away.**
1. *Security is a probability statement.* A system is perfectly secret when seeing the ciphertext leaves the attacker's probabilities for every message unchanged. Lesson 25's check computes exactly this:
   ```
   3-bit OTP, non-uniform prior: posterior = prior for 64/64 (m, c) pairs
   2-bit key on 3-bit messages: 32 of 32 possible (m, c) pairs have posterior != prior; e.g. Pr[M=7 | C=7] = 4/13 vs prior 2/9
   ```
2. *Perfect secrecy is expensive.* It needs at least as many keys as messages — so a key as long as all the traffic, never reused. That is why every lab used computational schemes instead.
3. *Redundancy leaks.* Because English is predictable, a ciphertext of a few tens of letters can pin down a simple-substitution key (the unicity distance). Problem 27-P3 recomputes it: `log10(26!) = 26.61 | unicity distance H/0.7 = 38.0 letters` — higher than the paper's rounded estimate, but the same order of magnitude, which is all the argument needs.

**How it connects to the labs.** Nonce reuse in **Misuse you can test: Wycheproof vectors, timing and nonce reuse** is pad reuse; lesson 24's entropy is Shannon's tool; the decision to use AEADs with short keys is the practical-secrecy choice Shannon describes.

## Paper 2: Diffie & Hellman, "New Directions in Cryptography" (1976)
**Read first.** Section I (Introduction) for the two problems they set: key distribution among many users without couriers, and signatures that a third party can check. Then Section III on public key cryptography — the definitions of a public-key cryptosystem and of a public key distribution system, and the exponentiation-based key exchange: each party publishes g raised to a secret exponent mod a prime, and both compute the same shared value. Then Section IV on one-way authentication, which is the idea behind digital signatures.

**Skim or skip on a first pass.** Section II's survey of conventional cryptography (you know this from L100), the later sections relating problem classes and trap doors in complexity-theoretic terms, and the historical perspective at the end. Return to the complexity discussion after lesson 25: it is where the authors admit what they cannot prove.

**Three ideas to take away.**
1. *Public keys separate encryption from decryption*, so n users need n public keys instead of about n²/2 shared ones. The key distribution problem disappears; the problem of knowing whose public key is whose (their "public file" that must not be modified) appears — that is lesson 9's CA.
2. *Key exchange from a one-way function.* Lesson 23's toy reproduces it:
   ```
   DH p=23 g=5 a=6 b=15: A = 8, B = 19, B^a = 2, A^b = 2
   ```
   Both sides reach 2; an eavesdropper sees 8 and 19. The paper argues security *if* discrete logs are hard and says plainly that this is not proven — lesson 25 calls it the CDH/DL assumption.
3. *Security becomes a cost ratio.* Their estimate for a 200-bit modulus: about 400 multiplications for the user, about 2¹⁰⁰ for the attacker. Problem 27-P2: `2^100 = 1.268e+30 | log2(2^100/400) = 91.36`, a ratio of about 2⁹¹. Index-calculus algorithms later cut the attacker's cost far below that, which is why finite-field DH now needs 2048+ bits and lab 8 uses X25519.

**How it connects to the labs.** The 1976 exchange is unauthenticated: a man in the middle runs one exchange with each side. TLS on localhost fixed that with signatures and certificates; X25519 + HKDF is the same exchange with a modern group and a KDF; ML-KEM (lesson 18) is the response to the threat the paper could not foresee, Shor's algorithm.

## Common confusions
- **Q:** "Shannon proved strong encryption is impossible without huge keys." — **A:** He proved that for *perfect* secrecy; he also defined practical secrecy as work for the enemy, which is what AES provides.
- **Q:** "Diffie and Hellman proved their key exchange secure." — **A:** They conjectured it, explicitly; lesson 25 names the assumption.
- **Q:** "The 1976 protocol is what TLS does." — **A:** TLS adds authentication (lesson 9–10) and a KDF over the transcript (lesson 8); without them a man in the middle wins.
