# Ethics: encryption, lawful access and responsible disclosure — explained

## The big picture
Every lab here made data harder to read without a key — which is why courts and victims sometimes see strong encryption as an obstacle to justice. This lesson asks what an engineer should do when a lawful authority asks for access, and with a vulnerability they find. Connections: **Threat modelling cryptographic systems: STRIDE, attack trees and tests** gives the language to ask "who else could use this access tool?"; **Keys in hardware-style storage: PKCS#11, non-exportable keys and key ceremonies** showed how one signing key becomes a single point of failure; **Foundations II: probability for cryptography** explains why a 4-digit passcode survives only because guessing is rate-limited; and **Misuse you can test: Wycheproof vectors, timing and nonce reuse** is the kind of finding that triggers a disclosure decision.

The case is Apple and the FBI (San Bernardino, 2016): a court ordered Apple, under the All Writs Act of 1789, to build a signed iOS version that removed the passcode retry limits and auto-erase on one phone so the FBI could guess the passcode electronically. Apple refused publicly; the FBI later got in through an outside party and withdrew its filing. A 2018 DOJ Inspector General report found poor internal coordination and that the filing had relied partly on the claim that Apple's help was necessary.

## The technical core, in numbers
The device's AES encryption was never the target. The request removed the *policy layer* that rate-limits guesses. Lesson 24's lab measured why that layer matters for human-chosen secrets:
```
4-digit PIN where '1234' has 10%: Shannon 12.43 bits, min-entropy 3.32 bits
```
Even a perfectly uniform 4-digit passcode has only 10,000 possibilities (log₂ 10,000 ≈ 13.3 bits) — trivial to enumerate electronically once delays and erase are gone. So "only unlock this phone" really means "build and sign firmware that defeats the guessing limit", and whoever holds that firmware and the key that signs it holds the capability for every compatible device.

## Worked analysis with one framework: the ACM Code of Ethics (2018)
**Step 1 — Separate facts from claims.** Fact: the order required custom, Apple-signed firmware disabling auto-erase and delays and allowing electronic passcode entry for one device. Claim (Apple): the technique could be reused on any iPhone. Claim (FBI): Apple's help was necessary. Finding (OIG 2018): no evidence of an FBI capability at the time of testimony, but coordination failures delayed the outside solution. *Why this step:* reasoning on wrong facts gives confident wrong answers.

**Step 2 — Name stakeholders.** Victims and families (justice); FBI, DOJ, courts (investigation, precedent); Apple engineers (compelled work); hundreds of millions of users, including journalists and abuse survivors (security); other governments (would ask for the same); exploit vendors; legislators. *Why:* ACM 1.1 asks you to consider everyone affected, not just the parties in court.

**Step 3 — Identify the principles that apply.** ACM 1.2 *Avoid harm*: harm to victims from unreached evidence, harm to users from a weakening tool. 1.6 *Respect privacy*. 2.3 *Know and respect existing rules* — including the right to challenge an order through legal channels. 2.9 *Design and implement systems that are robustly and usably secure*. 3.7 *Recognise when a system is integrated into the infrastructure of society*. *Why:* naming principles makes the conflict explicit.

**Step 4 — Apply the threat model (lesson 21) to the requested artefact.** Asset: a signed firmware image that removes guessing limits. Adversaries: insiders, thieves of the image or signing key, any government able to compel the same tool. Blast radius: every device that accepts that signature. This is "Keys Under Doormats" in miniature. *Why:* it turns "is one phone different from a backdoor?" into a question about assets and adversaries.

**Step 5 — List options.** (a) Comply fully. (b) Refuse and challenge in court and in public (what Apple did). (c) Help with data the provider already legitimately holds (for example cloud backups) without weakening devices. (d) Ask legislators to define any access requirement in statute, open to public technical analysis. *Why:* ethics chooses among real alternatives.

**Step 6 — Weigh and decide.** 2.9 and 3.7 weigh heavily against building a general weakening mechanism; 2.3 is satisfied by challenging the order lawfully rather than ignoring it; 1.2 is honoured by option (c) and by supporting targeted lawful process. Decision: refuse to build the tool, support targeted access that does not lower security for all users, and push the question to legislators. **Honest cost:** some evidence becomes unreachable, and victims bear that cost. *Why:* a defensible decision states what it gives up.

## A contrasting view
A rule-of-law (and consequentialist) reading starts elsewhere: in a democracy, courts — not companies — decide what evidence may be reached, and a warrant-proof device places some crimes beyond judicial process. On this view Apple substituted its own judgement for a court's, and the "any iPhone" risk was speculative: Apple already guards the signing key for every iOS update, so one more signed image is a managed risk, not a new class of risk. The best version of this view also notes that the actual outcome — the FBI buying an undisclosed vulnerability — left *every* user exposed to a bug Apple could not fix, which is worse for privacy than a supervised, court-limited tool. The disagreement is about which risk is larger: concentrated legitimate access or a grey market of exploits. Coordinated-disclosure norms (ISO/IEC 29147, the CERT Guide) side with users here: a vulnerability withheld for access is a vulnerability left open for everyone.

## Worked problems
**26-P2 (why the retry limit matters more than AES).**
1. AES-256's key is derived from the passcode and a device-bound secret, so attacking AES directly is infeasible. *Why:* 2²⁵⁶ keys (lesson 25: no generic shortcut).
2. The passcode has low entropy: ≈ 13.3 bits for 4 uniform digits, and min-entropy much lower for human choices (`min-entropy 3.32 bits` above). *Why:* lesson 24 — min-entropy predicts the attacker's best guess.
3. Without delays and erase, an attacker enumerates 10,000 guesses electronically. *Why:* the security rested on rate limiting, the only thing the order removed.

**26-P4 (nonce reuse in a popular library — a disclosure plan).**
1. Confirm and minimise: reproduce with a test (lesson 15's `scan_for_reuse` pattern) without touching real users' data. *Why:* evidence, not speculation.
2. Contact the maintainers privately via their security policy (SECURITY.md or a private advisory); if none, a coordinator such as CERT/CC. *Why:* ISO/IEC 29147 — the vendor fixes first.
3. Agree a timeline (commonly about 90 days), a CVE, and a fix that makes nonces random or counter-based across restarts. *Why:* users need a patch before attackers learn the bug.
4. Publish after the fix, with upgrade guidance. *Why:* the CERT Guide's goal is reducing harm.

## Common confusions
- **Q:** "The FBI asked Apple to break encryption." — **A:** It asked Apple to remove guessing limits; the passcode's low entropy did the rest.
- **Q:** "Refusing a court order is always unethical." — **A:** ACM 2.3 includes challenging rules through legitimate channels, which is what happened.
- **Q:** "Buying an exploit avoided the dilemma." — **A:** It left an unpatched vulnerability in every user's device — the disclosure problem in another form.
