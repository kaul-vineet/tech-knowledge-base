## Overview
The capstone builds one service from the whole topic: an **encrypted document service** that stores contracts, lets listed readers open them, shares them with an external recipient, and proves afterwards who did what. It runs end to end on 127.0.0.1, with seeded faults. `run_capstone.py` prints a JSON-lines scorecard of six milestones, and deterministic code decides every verdict. A local model only explains audit anomalies, and its explanations are checked too. The design follows the LPC1768 guide's capstone spirit: the core decides, AI explains.

## Architecture walkthrough
(Diagram: `labs/L400/22-capstone/architecture.mmd`.)
1. **Put.** The document service creates a fresh data key, seals the document in lesson 17's versioned envelope (the header names the AEAD and is the AAD), and signs it through the token's `doc-signing` **handle** (lesson 19). It asks the KMS to wrap the data key. The store keeps only `{envelope, KEK id, wrapped key}`.
2. **KMS over mTLS.** The KMS holds lesson 12's versioned key-encryption keys and answers only over mutual TLS (lessons 9-10). The caller's identity is its certificate CN. Rotation adds a version, a resumable rewrap job moves wrapped keys, and retirement is refused while any record needs the version. In M1 the seeded crash left `{"docs-kek:v1": 3, "docs-kek:v2": 3}`, retire was refused, the job resumed, and 6/6 documents still opened. In M2, clients without a certificate, or with one from another CA, were refused.
3. **Signed audit log.** Every put, get, share, revoke and KMS call is a hash-chained entry signed through the token's `audit-log` handle. Rules in `audit.py` decide anomalies: an unwrap burst, access after revocation, a broken chain, a bad signature, a sequence gap. In M3 the clean 50-entry log showed exactly the two seeded behaviour anomalies, and the attacker's edited-and-truncated copy added three integrity findings.
4. **PQ-hybrid sharing.** An external recipient gets the data key through HPKE with ML-KEM-768 + X25519 (lesson 18). The wrapped key is 1,168 bytes, and new shares with the deprecated X25519 KEM are refused (M4).
5. **Fitness and secrets.** Lesson 17's agility lint (0 violations), lesson 19's no-key-export checks, lesson 21's threat-model checker over `threat_model.json` (0 problems), and lesson 16's leak scanner over the redacted JSON logs (0 findings, even after a seeded line carrying a bearer token and the PIN) all run in M5. The token PIN comes from a file, never from code.
6. **Agent explains.** M6 sends only anomaly lines through the secret gate. The fake model explains all seven anomalies and invents an eighth (A99), which is rejected; paging on-call is decided by code.

## How it ties L100–L400
L100 gives the primitives the service never reimplements: AEAD with nonces and AAD, hashing, secure randomness. L200 adds signatures, hybrid encryption, the local CA, TLS and tokens, which become the envelope signatures, the KMS PKI and the mTLS channel. L300 hardens them: rotation with retire-after-rewrap, a strict verifier mindset, misuse tests, and redaction with a log scanner. L400 turns them into design decisions with fitness functions: agility (the registry and the lint), post-quantum hybrids (sharing), non-exportable keys (handles), group-key reasoning (epochs and removal, the same "fresh entropy excludes the removed party" idea as revocation), and a threat model whose controls point at the very tests that run here. Four ADRs record the capstone's choices, and the scorecard is the receipt.
