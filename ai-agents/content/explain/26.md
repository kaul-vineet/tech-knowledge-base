# Ethics, society and professional responsibility for AI agents — explained

## The big picture
Every technical control in this course also answers an ethical question. Citations in "Retrieval-augmented answers" are honesty. Approval queues in "Safe tool execution" are accountability. Retention in "Governance and evaluation in production" is privacy. "Deterministic core, LLM at the edge" decides who is responsible for a decision. This lesson makes the ethical reasoning explicit, using a real case: **Moffatt v. Air Canada (2024 BCCRT 149)**. An airline's website chatbot told a bereaved customer he could claim a bereavement fare after buying full-price tickets; the policy page said otherwise. The airline argued it was not liable for its chatbot. The tribunal disagreed: the chatbot is part of the airline's website, the airline is responsible for everything on it, it did not take reasonable care, and the customer's reliance was reasonable. Your company is about to launch a refund-and-policy agent built from lessons 7–22. The task: review it in the light of the case.

## Worked analysis with one framework: the ACM Code of Ethics (2018)
The ACM Code is written for computing professionals, so it fits an engineering review. Walk it as five steps.

**Step 1 — Name the stakeholders and what each stands to lose.** The lesson lists them: customers relying on answers (some in distress, with little time), the deploying company (liability, reputation), support staff who review output (workload, automation bias), engineers and product owners (professional duty), model and tool providers, regulators and tribunals, and people whose personal data sits in prompts and traces. *Why this step:* the Code's first principle (1.1) is about all people affected, not only the paying customer.

**Step 2 — Identify which principles the agent touches.**
- **1.3 Be honest and trustworthy** — includes not misrepresenting an organisation's policies. A wrong refund answer *is* a misrepresentation, however fluent.
- **1.2 Avoid harm** — the bereaved customer lost money relying on the answer.
- **2.5 Give comprehensive and thorough evaluations** — including the risks of machine-learning systems.
- **2.9 Design robustly and usably secure systems** — and the Code's guidance not to implement when harm is predictable and unmitigated.
- **1.6 Respect privacy** — transcripts and memories hold personal data.
*Why this step:* it turns "is this OK?" into specific, checkable obligations.

**Step 3 — Ask what each principle requires *before* launch.** 1.3 + 2.5: evaluate entitlement answers against the authoritative policy — a golden set of policy questions (lesson 12) including rare, costly ones like retroactive bereavement claims. 1.3: answers must be generated from, and checked against, the policy text (lesson 7's grounded citations), not merely link to it — the case shows a "see the policy page" link is no defence for a wrong affirmative statement. 1.2: entitlements are decisions; per lesson 20, code decides from policy and the model explains. 1.6: retention limits on transcripts (lesson 21).

**Step 4 — Quantify where the obligations conflict with cost.** Human review is the obvious fix, but it costs money and humans get complacent. The lesson's problem l26-p3 makes this concrete (10,000 questions a month, 2% wrong, 200 per wrong answer). **See it in numbers** — my run:
```
errors/month 200; A no review 40,000; B review all 19,000; C targeted (catch 0.9) 15,700; C' targeted (catch 0.5) 28,500
```
Targeted review of the 30% of traffic that holds 80% of errors is cheapest (15,700) — *if* reviewers stay vigilant. If automation bias drops their catch rate to 50%, the same design costs 28,500. The vigilance assumption is worth 12,800 a month, so the Code's 2.5 obligation extends to *measuring* reviewer catch rates (e.g. with seeded errors), not assuming them.

**Step 5 — Decide and record.** Under the Code, the defensible launch is: code-decided entitlements with grounded explanations; targeted human review of refund/policy answers with measured catch rates; an honest "I can't confirm this — here is how to get a binding answer" for questions outside the policy; and a record (lesson 21) of policy versions and agent outputs. The case shows why the record matters: the tribunal's figures (damages 1,630.36 − 979.48 = 650.88 CAD; 812.02 CAD with interest and fees, which my run reproduces) were decided partly because the airline produced no evidence of the actual fare. Logs are legal evidence.

## A contrasting view: pure cost-benefit
A consequentialist manager might say: "Option C costs 15,700, option A costs 40,000, so we review the risky slice and accept the residual 56 wrong answers a month as a cost of doing business." That view agrees with the ACM analysis on the *design* but disagrees on two points. First, it treats harm as fungible: a 200 average hides that some wrong answers fall on people in distress who cannot absorb the loss — the Code's 1.2 and the NIST AI RMF's risk tolerance ask whether *any* class of answer has unacceptable harm, in which case the agent should not answer it at all ("cease in a safe manner"). Second, it prices only measurable costs; the tribunal also weighed reasonable reliance — the customer had no way to know which part of the website to trust. Cost-benefit is a useful input to Step 4, not a substitute for Steps 2–3.

## Common confusions
**Q:** A disclaimer or a link to the policy shifts responsibility to the user. — **A:** In Moffatt it did not; the deployer answers for what its agent says.

**Q:** Adding human review solves accountability. — **A:** Only if reviewers stay vigilant; C' shows automation bias can make review worse than reviewing everything.

**Q:** Ethics is a separate step after engineering. — **A:** The controls *are* the ethics: citations (1.3), approvals (1.2), retention (1.6), evals (2.5).
