# Reading seminar: Attention Is All You Need and ReAct — explained

## The big picture
Two papers sit underneath everything you ran. The Transformer (Vaswani et al., 2017) is the model behind every `/v1/chat/completions` call since "Call a model over HTTP"; its attention cost is why context length drives cost in "State and memory" and "Cost, latency and model choice". ReAct (Yao et al., ICLR 2023) is the thought–action–observation loop you wrote in "A minimal agent loop". Reading the originals lets you separate what was *measured* from what was *hoped*, which is the skill lessons 24 and 25 built. Both papers are on arXiv; this guide summarises and points — it does not reproduce their text. Read them there.

## How to use this guide
Three passes per paper, as the concept note suggests: **map** (abstract, figures, tables, conclusion; write the claim in one sentence), **mechanism** (the method section with a pen; re-derive one equation), **critique** (for each headline number: compared with what, on how many examples, with what variance).

## Paper 1 — Attention Is All You Need
**Read first.** The abstract and Section 1 for the problem (recurrent models compute step by step, limiting parallel training). Then Section 3.2 on scaled dot-product and multi-head attention — this is equation (1), the same formula as `attention()` in `tiny_lm.py`. Then Section 4 and its Table 1, which compare layer types by per-layer cost, sequential operations and path length.

**Skim or skip on a first read.** The training details in Section 5 (hardware, optimiser schedule, regularisation), the encoder–decoder plumbing of 3.1 beyond the diagram, and the constituency-parsing transfer in 6.3. Come back to them only if you implement or train a model.

**Three ideas to take away.**
1. *Attention replaces recurrence.* Every position attends to every other in one step, so training parallelises and distant tokens are one hop apart.
2. *Scaling by √d_k matters.* Section 3.2.1 gives the variance argument you proved as lesson 23's T2 — but note the authors say they *suspect* small softmax gradients are the reason; your check measured it.
3. *Cost is quadratic in sequence length.* Self-attention is O(n²·d) per layer vs O(n·d²) for recurrence; it was cheaper for the sentence lengths of the time, and the crossover is exactly why long agent contexts are expensive.

**See it in numbers** (problem l27-p1, my run):
```
d_k = 64; scale 1/sqrt(d_k) = 0.1250; n^2 d = 5,120,000; n d^2 = 26,214,400; ratio = 5.12; equal at n = d = 512
```
With d_model = 512 and 8 heads, each head has d_k = 64 and scale 0.1250. At n = 100 tokens attention is 5.12× cheaper than recurrence; at n = 512 they tie. A 32k-token agent context is far past the crossover — which is why lesson 19's KV-cache sizing and lesson 9's summarise-to-fit exist.

**How it connects to the labs.** `tiny_lm.attention()` is equation (1) with an optional causal mask; lesson 19's `sizing.py` prices the KV cache this architecture needs; lesson 17's context overflow is n² cost meeting a fixed window.

## Paper 2 — ReAct: Synergizing Reasoning and Acting in Language Models
**Read first.** Section 2, where the action space is augmented with free-form "thoughts" that change the context but not the environment. Then one full example trajectory in the figures — rewrite it as your lesson-6 loop (thought → tool call → observation). Then the knowledge-intensive results (HotpotQA, FEVER) and the human-labelled analysis of success and failure modes (Table 2).

**Skim or skip on a first read.** The fine-tuning experiments and most of the appendix prompts; the decision-making benchmarks (ALFWorld, WebShop) can be skimmed for the pattern rather than the numbers.

**Three ideas to take away.**
1. *Acting grounds reasoning.* Looking things up reduces hallucinated facts compared with chain-of-thought alone — the motivation for lesson 7's retrieval and lesson 8's tools.
2. *Acting can also trap reasoning.* The authors report the model repeating earlier thoughts and actions — lesson 25's p = 0 stuck loop, which lesson 6's `max_steps` and lesson 14's budgets bound.
3. *Safety by construction.* The experiments use a deliberately weak, read-only action space (search, lookup, finish), and the ethics statement limits work to environments without dangerous actions — the allow-list idea of lessons 11 and 16, and the approval gates of lessons 15 and 20 for anything that acts.

**See it in numbers** (problem l27-p3: Table 2's false-positive rates treated as 3/50 and 7/50, my run):
```
ReAct 3/50 Wilson [0.0206, 0.1622]  CoT 7/50 Wilson [0.0695, 0.2619]  unpaired p 0.1824
```
The intervals overlap heavily and p = 0.1824: with 50 labelled trajectories per group, the direction favours ReAct but the difference is not established. The authors present this analysis qualitatively; read it that way.

**How it connects to the labs.** Lesson 6's `run_agent()` is a ReAct loop without visible thoughts; lesson 13's traces are its observable trajectory; lesson 17's single agent "adapts to observations" — the exact property ReAct argues for and the planner lacked on t09.

## Common confusions
**Q:** The Transformer paper proved why √d_k scaling works. — **A:** It gives the variance argument and *suspects* the gradient explanation; lesson 23's check measured it.

**Q:** ReAct shows thoughts make agents safer. — **A:** Safety came from the read-only action space; thoughts improve grounding, and loops still need budgets.

**Q:** A 6% vs 14% gap on 50 examples is a clear win. — **A:** Wilson intervals overlap; p = 0.1824 unpaired.
