# Reading seminar: Parnas (1972) and the SEI ATAM report (2000) — explained

## The big picture
Two papers sit underneath most of this course. Parnas (1972) gives the criterion behind every boundary you enforced: "Modular monolith boundaries with a fitness function", "Hexagonal architecture: ports and adapters" and "Strangler-fig decomposition: facade, branch by abstraction, parallel run, cut-over" all hide a decision behind an interface. The SEI ATAM report (Kazman, Klein & Clements, 2000) gives the vocabulary of "Foundations II: quality attributes and trade-off analysis – ISO/IEC 25010, ATAM and availability maths": utility trees, scenarios, risks, sensitivity and trade-off points. Both are routinely misquoted — Parnas as "small modules are good", ATAM as "a scoring method".

This guide summarises; it does not reproduce. Both texts are copyrighted (ACM; Carnegie Mellon University) though free to read — paraphrase and cite.

## Reading guide 1: Parnas, "On the criteria to be used in decomposing systems into modules" (CACM, 1972)
**Read first:** the KWIC example and the two decompositions. Parnas describes a small index-producing program (Key Word In Context) and decomposes it twice: once by processing steps (input, circular shift, alphabetise, output — the flowchart way), once by design decisions, each hidden in a module. Draw both as boxes first; the argument lives in that contrast.

**Read second:** the comparison of the two decompositions — changeability, independent development, comprehensibility — and the list of design decisions likely to change (input format, keeping all lines in memory, packing characters, building an index of shifts, alphabetising once vs on demand). This is where the paper's evidence lives. Notice what kind of evidence it is: a careful *argument* on one example, not an experiment.

**Read third:** the stated criterion near the end, and the section on efficiency and implementation, where Parnas concedes that a naive implementation of the second decomposition could be slower because of many procedure calls, and sketches how it could be implemented efficiently.

**Skim or skip on a first pass:** the shorter closing sections (a decomposition shared by a compiler and an interpreter, and hierarchical structure). They are worth a second reading once the main idea is solid, because they separate "hides a decision" from "uses another module" — two different relations.

**Three ideas to take away:**
1. The criterion: start from decisions that are hard or likely to change, and give each to one module that hides it behind an interface revealing as little as possible.
2. Processing steps are a poor basis for modules: a flowchart decomposition spreads format and representation decisions across many modules, so one change touches many.
3. Interfaces should be chosen so that change stays local — modularity is judged by *what a change costs*, not by how the code looks.

**See it in the lab:** lesson 23's `check_modularity.py` encodes our reading of the KWIC argument (a model, which you should check against the paper):
```
Worked example: Parnas KWIC change impact (modules that must change per design decision)
      conventional        [1, 4, 4, 3, 2] total 14, worst 4
      information hiding  [1, 1, 1, 1, 1] total 5, worst 1
```
One change per decision versus up to four. The caveat matters: these "knows-sets" are our model, and the count ignores how big each change is.

**How it connects to the labs:** lesson 3's `internal/` packages hide the catalogue's storage; lesson 7's ports hide the storage technology from the domain; lesson 17's `FinesPort` hides *which* fines engine answers, which is what made the cut-over a routing change. Lesson 9's `asyncapi.json` is the opposite case — a published event shape is a decision deliberately *not* hidden, which is why renaming `bookId` broke every subscriber.

## Reading guide 2: Kazman, Klein & Clements, "ATAM: Method for Architecture Evaluation" (SEI technical report, 2000)
**Read first:** the purpose and assumptions — what ATAM is for and what it does not try to do: it finds risks in architectural decisions relative to quality goals early; it does not promise precise predictions.

**Read second:** the outputs and the core vocabulary — utility tree, scenarios (use-case, growth, exploratory), risks, non-risks, sensitivity points, trade-off points. Write each in one sentence of your own before moving on.

**Read third:** the steps and phases, focusing on *who* takes part in which step: the value comes from architects and broad stakeholders prioritising scenarios together.

**Skim or skip on a first pass:** the attribute-specific question lists and the long example evaluation. Return to them when you run an evaluation yourself; they are reference material, not argument.

**Three ideas to take away:**
1. Qualities must be made concrete as scenarios with a stimulus and a measurable response — "highly available" is not evaluable, "restored within 5 min" is.
2. Prioritise with a utility tree by importance and difficulty (H/M/L), and spend analysis on the (H, H) leaves.
3. The useful output is not a score but the list of risks, sensitivity points and trade-off points, tied to specific decisions.

**See it in the lab:** lesson 24's `check_quality.py` prints a utility tree from the course:
```
        (H,H) availability: Primary database fails at peak; service restored within 5 min, no comm...
        (H,M) availability: Identity provider is down; members already signed in can still renew l...
```
and the trade-off maths: `SQLite (0.4, 0.9) vs replicated PostgreSQL (0.95, 0.5): w* = 0.4211`. The weight on availability is a sensitivity point; the storage choice is a trade-off point.

**How it connects to the labs:** each L400 lesson is a miniature ATAM: a design brief with constraints (the scenarios), measured options, an ADR naming risks and consequences, and a fitness function. The capstone scorecard is what the report's analysis looks like when automated and re-run every release. What automation cannot replace is the stakeholder breadth in the middle steps.

## Worked problems
**P27-1 — Parnas's criterion in one sentence.**
1. Find the unit of analysis: a *design decision*, not a processing step. *Why:* that is the paper's contrast.
2. Find the selection rule: decisions that are difficult or likely to change.
3. Find the mechanism: one module hides each, behind an interface that reveals as little as possible.
4. Combine: "List the decisions most likely to change and give each to one module that hides it behind a minimal interface."

**P27-2 — sensitivity and trade-off points, with Library Loans examples.**
1. Sensitivity point: a property one quality response depends on strongly. Example: the database's availability bounds the whole request path (T24.1: A ≤ min Aᵢ; a 3rd API replica only moved 99.8376 % to 99.8401 %). *Why this example:* the measurement shows the dependence.
2. Trade-off point: a property that is a sensitivity point for several qualities in opposite directions. Example: replicating the database raises availability but lowers simplicity and adds consistency questions — the w* = 0.4211 flip.
3. *Why the distinction matters:* a sensitivity point needs monitoring; a trade-off point needs a stakeholder decision recorded in an ADR.

## Common confusions
**Q:** Parnas says modules should be small. — **A:** He says modules should hide decisions; size is not the criterion.

**Q:** ATAM produces a score for the architecture. — **A:** It produces risks, sensitivity and trade-off points; the w* sweep shows why a single score would hide a judgement.

**Q:** Free access means I can paste passages into my notes for the team wiki. — **A:** Paraphrase with a citation; quote only short passages where the exact words matter, with author, year and section.
