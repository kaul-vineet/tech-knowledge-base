# Foundations III: random number generation and Monte Carlo theory — explained

## The big picture

Every lab consumed random numbers it never questioned. "Set up your simulation workbench" showed a seed makes a stream repeatable, but not that the stream is any good. "Performance and scale: vectorise, parallelise, keep streams independent" needed streams that do not overlap. "Variance reduction: same precision, fewer runs" measured an antithetic gain of 1.46x and a symmetric model that got *worse*. This lesson explains when a recurrence visits every state (and how its structure can still be fatal), what generator tests prove, why Monte Carlo error is σ/√N in any dimension, and when antithetic pairs help.

### Theorem 25.1 - Full period of an LCG (Hull-Dobell) and the RANDU planes

**In plain words** — x ← (ax + c) mod m visits all m states iff c is coprime to m, a − 1 is divisible by every prime factor of m, and by 4 if 4 divides m. A full period still says nothing about how *consecutive* values relate.

**Why it is true (intuition)** — if c shares a factor d with m, from 0 you only reach multiples of d; the other conditions make every prime-power residue cycle fully. Skeleton:
1. Necessity of (i): with gcd(c, m) = d > 1, iterates from 0 stay multiples of d.
2. Sufficiency and the rest: Hull & Dobell (1962), cited.
3. Check exhaustively for small m instead.
4. RANDU: a² ≡ 6a − 9 (mod 2³¹), so x₂ = 6x₁ − 9x₀: every triple lies on one of 15 planes.

**See it in numbers** — `python t1_lcg_period.py`:
```
m = 64: 4096 (a, c) pairs, full period for 512; Hull-Dobell predicts all 4096/4096
m = 60: 3600 (a, c) pairs, full period for 16; Hull-Dobell predicts all 3600/3600
RANDU triples violating x2 = 6 x1 - 9 x0 (mod 2^31): 0 of 99998; distinct planes used: 15
lowest bit of an m = 2^32 LCG (a = 1664525, c = 1013904223): 10101010101010101 -> period 2
```
Hull–Dobell predicts all 14,257 small cases; RANDU obeys its recurrence on every triple; a full-period LCG's low bit just alternates.

**Where it shows up in practice** — why lesson 1 uses PCG64, not a textbook LCG.

### Theorem 25.2 - Goodness-of-fit tests for generators

**In plain words** — under a perfect generator, Pearson's X² over k equal cells averages exactly k − 1 and tends to χ²(k − 1); the scaled KS distance tends to a fixed distribution.

**Why it is true (intuition)** — each cell's squared deviation over its mean averages 1 − pⱼ; the k terms sum to k − 1. Skeleton:
1. E[(Oⱼ − npⱼ)²] = npⱼ(1 − pⱼ).
2. Divide and sum: E[X²] = k − 1 exactly.
3. Multivariate CLT: the standardised counts are normal with a rank-(k − 1) projection covariance.
4. So X² → χ²(k − 1).

**See it in numbers** — `python t2_gof_tests.py`:
```
mean X^2 over 400 PCG64 runs (k = 100): 99.06 (theory k - 1 = 99)
generator  1-D X^2 (99 df)  p      KS sqrt(n)D  p      3-D X^2 (7999 df)  p
PCG64      87.8             0.782  1.064       0.208  7868.5             0.849
RANDU      76.1             0.958  0.636       0.814  151465.4           0
```
RANDU *passes* the 1-D and KS tests and fails the 3-D test catastrophically — the planes of 25.1.

**Where it shows up in practice** — the KS distance you used to rank service-time fits in lesson 7.

### Theorem 25.3 - The Monte Carlo estimator, its 1/sqrt(N) error, and importance sampling

**In plain words** — the sample mean of f is unbiased with standard error σ/√N regardless of dimension; sampling from a better-placed q and reweighting by p/q stays unbiased and can shrink σ enormously.

**Why it is true (intuition)** — linearity gives unbiasedness; independence adds variances. Reweighting is a change of measure. Skeleton:
1. E[μ̂] = μ by linearity.
2. Var(μ̂) = σ²/N by independence — no dimension appears.
3. E_q[f·p/q] = E_p[f]: unbiased for any q > 0 where fp ≠ 0.
4. q* = fp/μ makes f·p/q constant: zero variance.

**See it in numbers** — `python t3_mc_variance_is.py`:
```
    100    0.05046                 0.04920         1.71770
    6400   0.00625                 0.00615         1.71844
    log-log slope of sd vs N: -0.500 (theory -0.5)
    naive,  N = 100,000: estimate 3.0000e-05; theory relative SE 0.562
    IS N(4,1), N = 10,000: estimate 3.1924e-05; sample relative SE 0.0212; theory 0.0212
    variance per sample: naive 3.167e-05 vs IS 4.525e-09 -> 6,999x fewer samples
```
The slope is exactly −0.5. For P(Z > 4) the naive estimate rests on 3 hits (relative SE 56%); shifting the proposal to N(4, 1) gives 2% error with a tenth of the samples.

**Where it shows up in practice** — lesson 2's half-width column, 0.436 → 0.015.

### Theorem 25.4 - Antithetic variates: the covariance argument

**In plain words** — averaging f(U) and f(1 − U) has variance (σ²/2)(1 + ρ); it beats two independent draws iff ρ < 0, which holds for every monotone f.

**Why it is true (intuition)** — U and 1 − U move in opposite directions; a monotone f carries that opposition into its outputs. Skeleton:
1. f(U) and f(1 − U) have the same variance σ².
2. Var((A + B)/2) = (σ²/2)(1 + ρ).
3. With an independent copy V, (f(U) − f(V))(g(U) − g(V)) ≤ 0 for g(u) = f(1 − u).
4. Taking expectations gives Cov ≤ 0.

**See it in numbers** — `python t4_antithetic.py`:
```
f(u) = e^u: rho = (e - (e-1)^2) / sigma^2 = -0.9677; predicted gain 1/(1 + rho) = 30.9x
  same 1,000 evaluations: var plain 2.384e-04, antithetic 7.922e-06, observed gain 30.1x
f(u) = (u - 0.5)^2 (symmetric): observed gain 0.53x (theory 1/(1 + 1) = 0.50x)
```
Prediction and observation agree; a symmetric f has ρ = +1 and *halves* efficiency.

**Where it shows up in practice** — lesson 12's 1.46x (ρ = −0.32) and the 0.49x symmetric try_it.

## Worked problems

**25-P4.** Counts 112, 95, 88, 104, 99, 91, 118, 97, 100, 96 in 10 cells.
1. Expected 100 each — *why:* 1,000 draws, equal cells.
2. X² = (144 + 25 + 144 + 16 + 1 + 81 + 324 + 9 + 0 + 16)/100 = 7.60 — *why:* Pearson's sum of squared deviations over expectation.
3. df = k − 1 = 9, p = 0.575 — *why:* Theorem 25.2's limit is χ²(k − 1).
4. Conclusion: no evidence against uniformity — *and its limit:* RANDU passes 1-D tests too.

**25-P7.** f(u) = eᵘ, ρ = −0.9677; evaluations for half-width 0.001?
1. Gain 1/(1 + ρ) = 30.9x — *why:* Theorem 25.4.
2. Pair variance (σ²/2)(1 + ρ) with σ² = 0.24204 — *why:* the pair mean is the sampling unit.
3. Pairs = 1.96² × 0.12102 × (1 − 0.9677) / 10⁻⁶ ≈ 15,031 with unrounded ρ, i.e. 30,062 evaluations — *why:* solve 1.96·sd/√pairs = 0.001.
4. Compare 929,804 for plain Monte Carlo (25-P5).

## Common confusions

**Q:** A long period means a good generator. — **A:** RANDU has a long period and 15 planes.

**Q:** Passing a test proves quality. — **A:** Tests can only reject; RANDU's 1-D p is 0.958.

**Q:** Importance sampling is biased. — **A:** The weights p/q correct it exactly; the estimate 3.1924e-05 sits within one SE of 3.1671e-05.
