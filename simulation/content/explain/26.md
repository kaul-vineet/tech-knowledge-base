# Ethics and professional responsibility: models that make decisions about people — explained

## The big picture

Every earlier lesson asked "is the model accurate enough *for its question*?". "Is the model right? Verification vs validation" separated code correctness from model validity; "Model governance and V&V: model cards checked against evidence" made the validity envelope a machine-checked claim. This lesson asks what happens when the question is about *people*, and the decision lands on each of them individually. The 2020 English exam-grading case is the worked example: a model that was validated at one level (the grade distribution of a school) was used to make decisions at another (one grade per named student). The professional failure was not mainly a coding error; it was a mismatch between the evidence and the use, poorly communicated.

The companion script makes that mismatch measurable on synthetic data — it does not reproduce Ofqual's model.

**See it in numbers** — `python aggregate_vs_individual.py`:
```
teacher-exam correlation 0.80: distribution error 0.0000; same grade as exam 40.0%; within one grade 86.4%
teacher-exam correlation 0.90: distribution error 0.0000; same grade as exam 51.5%; within one grade 95.3%
exam vs an equally good re-sit: same grade 39.7%; within one 86.5%
  n =   8: SD 0.090 (theory sqrt(p(1-p)/3n) = 0.090), relative error 34%
  n = 200: SD 0.018 (theory sqrt(p(1-p)/3n) = 0.018), relative error 7%
```
Read each line slowly. *Distribution error 0.0000*: ranking students and handing out grades in historical proportions reproduces the national distribution perfectly — by construction. *Same grade as exam 40.0%*: yet six students in ten get a different grade from the one an exam would have given. *Re-sit 39.7%*: exams themselves are noisy at the individual level, so "the exam grade" is not ground truth either. *n = 8, 34%*: a small centre's history is a poor estimate of its own grade shares — the law of large numbers working against small groups (Foundations I, Theorem 23.1).

## The case, analysed step by step

Framework: the **ACM Code of Ethics and Professional Conduct (2018)**, applied through the lesson's structured method (facts → intended use → stakeholders and harms → obligations → options → recommendation). The clause numbers below are those the lesson lists.

1. **Facts.** Exams were cancelled. Under a ministerial direction, Ofqual had to award grades that kept national standards in line with previous years. Teachers supplied a centre assessment grade (CAG) and a rank order. CAGs were optimistic; the model predicted each centre's distribution from its history and prior attainment and assigned grades down the rank order. Small cohorts (fewer than 15) leaned on CAGs. — *Why start here:* every later judgement must be traceable to a fact, not an impression.

2. **Intended use.** Two goals: comparability with past years (a *group* property) and fairness to each student (an *individual* property). The model was designed and back-tested for the first and then used for the second when a predicted distribution became one grade per name. — *Why this step:* Sargent's operational validity is relative to a stated use; the use quietly changed.

3. **Stakeholders and harms.** Students (a grade two steps off can cost a university place, irreversibly on results day); teachers (judgements overridden without explanation); small and large centres treated differently; universities relying on comparable grades; future cohorts and public trust in public-sector models. — *Why:* ACM 1.1 asks you to weigh effects on all people affected, especially the less powerful.

4. **Obligations.**
   - **ACM 1.2 (avoid harm):** foreseeable individual harm was large even when the aggregate was right — the script's 40.0% line is the kind of evidence an analyst could have produced in advance.
   - **ACM 1.3 (be honest and trustworthy) and 2.5 (comprehensive evaluations including risks):** "51 of 55 subjects had more than 90% within one grade" is true but omits what it means for one student. The regulator for statistics later found that limitations and uncertainty were not fully communicated.
   - **ACM 3.1 (public good central):** the small-cohort rule was statistically sound (34% relative error at n = 8) but systematically favoured students in small classes, because their optimistic CAGs carried more weight. That trade-off belonged in front of the decision maker.
   - *Why list clauses:* they turn "it felt unfair" into specific duties you can check documentation against.

5. **Options.** (a) Publish as designed. (b) Publish with individual-level uncertainty statements and a pre-designed appeal route. (c) Review predictable outliers by hand before release. (d) Use CAGs and accept inflation. (e) Delay. — *Why enumerate:* recommendations without alternatives cannot be evaluated.

6. **Recommendation.** Option (b) + (c): publish the method in advance, state individual uncertainty plainly, review students whose calculated grade is two or more steps below their CAG, and agree the small-cohort trade-off explicitly with the commissioner. — *Why:* it keeps the group goal while meeting 1.2, 1.3 and 2.5 at the individual level.

## A contrasting view

A standards-first (consequentialist) reading defends much of the original design. Awarding optimistic CAGs to everyone would have raised A* rates from 7.7% to 13.9% (the lesson's figure), making 2020 grades incomparable with neighbouring years and harming *future* cohorts and anyone relying on grades as signals. The script also shows that even a good exam agrees with a re-sit only 39.7% of the time: individual-level "error" exists every year and is tolerated. On this view the model did its assigned job and the failure was political — a commissioner's direction that could not be met for individuals.

The two views meet on communication. Even if the standards argument wins, ACM 1.3 and 2.5 still required stating that roughly 3.6% of grades differed from the CAG by two or more grades — about 26,000 A level entries — and designing review before release, not after.

## Common confusions

**Q:** A model with zero distribution error is accurate. — **A:** Distribution error 0.0000 coexists with 40.0% individual agreement.

**Q:** The small-cohort rule was a statistical mistake. — **A:** It was statistically sound (SD 0.090 at n = 8); the problem was an undisclosed distributional effect.

**Q:** Ethics is separate from V&V. — **A:** Validity is judged against an intended use; changing the use without new evidence is both a validation and an honesty failure.
