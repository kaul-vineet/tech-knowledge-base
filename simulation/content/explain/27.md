# Reading seminar: Metropolis & Ulam (1949) 'The Monte Carlo Method' and Sargent on verification and validation — explained

## The big picture

The two readings are the roots of the two halves of this course. Metropolis and Ulam named and explained the idea behind "Monte Carlo: estimate a project's completion time", "Variance reduction: same precision, fewer runs" and "Foundations III: random number generation and Monte Carlo theory": answer a hard probabilistic question by following many random histories and counting. Sargent's tutorial is the vocabulary behind "Is the model right? Verification vs validation", "Model governance and V&V: model cards checked against evidence" and the capstone: verification, validation, operational validity, and the rule that the accuracy requirement comes *before* the evidence.

Read them in that order. The first tells you why simulation answers are random variables; the second tells you how to decide whether such an answer is good enough to act on. This guide summarises; it does not reproduce either text — the lesson links the originals (JSTOR for the 1949 paper, a free PDF for Sargent).

**See it in numbers** — the companion check turns Metropolis & Ulam's "chains of events" into a branching process and applies a Sargent-style operational-validity test. `python branching_process.py`:
```
analytic extinction probability q = 0.6863 (smallest root of q = exp(1.2(q - 1)))
Monte Carlo, 20,000 chains: 0.6842 +/- 0.0064 (95% CI); contains q: True
capped chains contribute bias < q^500 = 1.8e-82
```
Each chain starts with one particle; each particle has Poisson(1.2) offspring; a chain either dies out or explodes. 68.42% of 20,000 chains died; the 95% interval contains the analytic 0.6863. The cap at 500 particles is harmless: a chain that large dies with probability below 1.8e-82.

## Reading guide

### Metropolis & Ulam (1949), "The Monte Carlo Method"

**Read first:** the opening, where the authors place the method between the mechanics of a few bodies (solved by equations) and of very many (solved by statistical mechanics) — the middle ground where neither works. Then the neutron-multiplication illustration: what the state of a particle is, what is sampled at each step, and how a frequency over many chains replaces a multiple integral.

**Skim or skip on a first pass:** the more formal remarks on the underlying equations; come back to them after Foundations I.

**Three ideas to take away:**
1. *Simulate the individual, count the population.* Rather than iterating probability matrices, follow single histories and read the answer as a frequency — exactly what `branching_process.py` does with 20,000 chains.
2. *Machines change what is tractable.* The method was proposed for the then-new electronic computers, whose speed makes many cheap histories better than one expensive calculation.
3. *The error question was open.* The authors say a precise error estimate for a finite sample remained to be worked out. Today that is σ/√N with a CLT interval (Theorems 23.2 and 25.3) — and it needs finite variance.

**How it connects to the labs:** the 1.96·s/√N half-width in lesson 2 is the answer to their open question; lesson 16's spawned streams and lesson 20's identity-keyed streams are what "run it on many machines" requires in practice; RANDU's planes (lesson 25) are what would have quietly broken their three-dimensional particle tracks.

### Sargent (2010), "Verification and Validation of Simulation Models"

**Read first:** the definitions of verification and validation, then the simplified model-development diagram linking the problem entity, conceptual model and computerized model. Those two pages organise everything else.

**Then:** the catalogue of validation techniques — degenerate tests, extreme-condition tests, comparison to other models, historical data validation, sensitivity analysis, traces — and match each to a lab you have run.

**Skim or skip on a first pass:** accreditation and documentation detail; return to them when you write a model card.

**Three ideas to take away:**
1. *Validity is relative to a purpose.* A model is valid for an intended application within a domain of applicability, not in general — the lesson-21 envelope.
2. *Fix the accuracy requirement before testing.* Otherwise you can always move the goalposts; lesson 21's try_it shows a 5% requirement shrinks the envelope from 0.85 to 0.5.
3. *Four kinds of evidence.* Conceptual model validity, computerized model verification, operational validity and data validity fail independently; a strong argument covers all four.

**How it connects to the labs:** lesson 6's hand cases are computerized verification; lesson 21's `verification.py` groups Sargent's degenerate and extreme-condition tests; capstone M2 is a degenerate test against Erlang C. The 2020 update by the same author (linked in the lesson) adds decision approaches and parsimony.

## Worked problems

**27-P2.** Poisson(m) offspring; extinction probability q.
1. Condition on the first generation: the line dies iff each child's line dies, independently, so q = E[q^X] = G(q) — *why:* independence lets the event split by children.
2. For Poisson(m), G(s) = e^(m(s−1)), so q = e^(m(q−1)) — *why:* the Poisson generating function.
3. Iterate qₙ₊₁ = G(qₙ) from 0 — *why:* qₙ = P(extinct by generation n) increases to the *smallest* fixed point; s = 1 is always a root but not the answer when m > 1. Result for m = 1.2: 0.6863.
4. Expected half-width 1.96·√(q(1 − q)/20,000) ≈ 0.0064 — *why:* a binomial proportion (Theorem 23.2). The run reports 0.6842 ± 0.0064, which contains 0.6863.

**27-P4.** Map Sargent's four types onto course labs.
1. *Conceptual model validity* — are the assumptions right for the purpose? Lesson 7: lognormal, not exponential, service times. *Why:* the assumption itself was tested against data.
2. *Computerized model verification* — does the code implement the model? Lesson 6's rule unit tests; the order checks in Foundations II. *Why:* they test code against the model, not against reality.
3. *Operational validity* — is the output accurate enough over the domain? Lesson 21: within 10% up to utilisation 0.85. *Why:* it compares output with a reference under a stated requirement.
4. *Data validity* — are the data adequate? Lesson 17's feed checks (late, duplicate, null, glitch). *Why:* bad inputs invalidate even a verified, valid model.

## Common confusions

**Q:** Metropolis & Ulam gave the √N error law. — **A:** They flagged the error estimate as open; Theorems 23.2 and 25.3 close it.

**Q:** Sargent provides a pass/fail test for validity. — **A:** He provides techniques and a process; the decision remains a judgement against a stated requirement.

**Q:** One validated model can be reused for any question. — **A:** Validity is tied to an intended use and domain; a new use needs new evidence (lesson 26).
