**Concept: the probability that makes simulation output mean something**

## Why it matters
Every number this course has printed with a "±" relies on two theorems, and every queue check relies on a third. L100 #2 said a Monte Carlo estimate is 17.334 ± 0.015 days. L100 #3 compared a simulated wait with 4.000 minutes. L200 #10 built t intervals from replications. If you cannot say which assumptions those numbers rest on, you cannot tell when they are wrong. This lesson states the results precisely, proves the ones that are short enough to prove, and checks every one in code.

## Definitions
- **Random variable, mean, variance.** X has mean μ = E[X] and variance σ² = E[(X − μ)²]. The sample mean of n draws is X̄ₙ = (X₁ + … + Xₙ)/n.
- **Convergence in probability.** Xₙ → c if P(|Xₙ − c| ≥ ε) → 0 for every ε > 0.
- **Convergence in distribution.** The CDF of Xₙ approaches a limit CDF.
- **Poisson process (rate λ).** A count N(t) with N(0) = 0 and independent, stationary increments. In a short interval dt it records one event with probability λ dt, and two or more with negligible probability.
- **Markov chain.** The next state depends only on the current one. In discrete time a stationary distribution π satisfies πP = π. In continuous time, with generator Q, it satisfies πQ = 0.

## Key results (proofs in the lesson's `theorems`)
1. **Weak law of large numbers (Theorem 23.1).** P(|X̄ₙ − μ| ≥ ε) ≤ σ²/(nε²). The proof is three lines. Markov's inequality gives Chebyshev's, and independence gives Var(X̄ₙ) = σ²/n. That is the whole reason averages settle down.
2. **Central limit theorem (Theorem 23.2).** With finite variance, (X̄ₙ − μ)/(σ/√n) tends to N(0, 1). This gives the *shape* of the error, and so the ±1.96 σ/√n interval. With a finite third moment the approach has rate O(1/√n) (Berry-Esseen).
3. **Exponential gaps (Theorem 23.3).** The Poisson axioms alone give P₀′(t) = −λP₀(t), so P(no event in [0, t]) = e^(−λt). Each gap between events is therefore Exponential(λ) and memoryless.
4. **Cut balance (Theorem 23.4).** In steady state, probability flows across any cut equally in both directions. For a birth-death process this gives λπₙ = μπₙ₊₁. For M/M/1 that means πₙ = (1 − ρ)ρⁿ and L = ρ/(1 − ρ), and Little's law gives W = 1/(μ − λ).

## Intuition
Chebyshev is a worst-case statement. It holds for *every* distribution with variance σ², so it is loose for any particular one: at n = 100 the bound is 0.25 and exponential data give 0.0427. The CLT is the opposite kind of statement. It is sharp in the middle of the distribution, but it is only a limit, so it can be poor in the tails at finite n. The Poisson process is what "events arrive independently at a constant rate" means mathematically, and the exponential distribution is its consequence, not an extra assumption. Cut balance is conservation of probability flow: in steady state, the rate at which the queue goes up from n must equal the rate at which it comes down to n.

## Worked example (all numbers from the lesson's check scripts)
- **LLN.** Exponential(1), ε = 0.2. Observed P(|X̄ − 1| ≥ 0.2) is 0.3220, 0.0427, 0.0003 and 0.0000 at n = 25, 100, 400 and 1600. The Chebyshev bounds are 1, 0.25, 0.0625 and 0.0156: always above, and far above.
- **CLT.** Standardised sums of exponentials give P(Z ≤ 1.96) = 0.9482, 0.9524, 0.9631, 0.9696 and 0.9728 for n = 1, 4, 16, 64 and 256 (the limit is 0.9750). The Kolmogorov distance falls from 0.159 to 0.012, roughly like the skewness 2/√n.
- **Poisson.** We built the process from Bernoulli(0.002) cells of width 0.001 with λ = 2, never drawing an exponential. The mean gap came out 0.5025 against 1/λ = 0.5, and P(T > 1) was 0.1375 against e⁻² = 0.1353. Memorylessness held: P(T > 1.5 | T > 0.5) = 0.1411.
- **M/M/1.** λ = 0.8, μ = 1. The formula gives π₀..π₄ = 0.2000, 0.1600, 0.1280, 0.1024, 0.0819. A 200-state generator solve agrees to 1e-12. A 1,000,000-transition simulation gives L = 3.919 against 4.000.

By hand: λ = 0.9, μ = 1 gives L = 9 and W = 10 minutes, and P(N ≥ 5) = 0.9⁵ = 0.59. A 12.5% rise in load (from 0.8) doubles the wait.

## Where the course uses it
- **L100 #2.** The ± came from the CLT, and the shrinkage 1/√N came from Var(X̄) = σ²/N.
- **L100 #3 and L200 #8.** The DES was verified against M/M/1 and Erlang C. The same cut-balance argument with μₙ = min(n, c)μ gives Erlang C.
- **L200 #7.** Exponential service was rejected by KS. Poisson *arrivals* are a separate assumption that often holds better than exponential *service*.
- **L200 #10.** As ρ → 1, the pole in L = ρ/(1 − ρ) means slow convergence. You need long warm-ups and many replications.
- **L400 #22.** Time-varying Poisson arrivals are generated by thinning, which is the same axioms with λ(t).

## Common misconceptions
- **"The law of large numbers says errors cancel quickly."** It only says they eventually become small. The *rate* (1/√n) comes from the variance, and with infinite variance there is no such rate.
- **"The CLT makes the data normal."** It makes the *mean* approximately normal. Individual service times stay as skewed as they were. Even the mean's tails converge slowly: for the sum of 100 exponentials, P(S > 130) is 0.00275, while the CLT gives 0.00135.
- **"Exponential gaps are an extra modelling choice on top of Poisson arrivals."** They are the same assumption: one implies the other (Theorem 23.3).
- **"A stationary distribution exists for any queue if you simulate long enough."** For ρ ≥ 1 in M/M/1 there is none. The normalising sum diverges and the queue grows without bound. A finite buffer (M/M/1/K) always has one.
- **"Steady-state formulas describe the first hour."** They describe the long run. Use warm-up (L200 #10) or transient analysis for start-up behaviour.
