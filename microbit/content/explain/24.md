# Foundations II: sampling and signal processing — explained

## The big picture
Every sensor lab turned a continuous world into numbers at discrete instants, then smoothed them. **Sensing temperature and light** read `read_light_level()` once a second; **Motion and gestures** polled a noisy accelerometer; **Data logging** chose a sampling interval; **Power budgeting (modelled, not measured)** stretched that interval to 8 s; **A digital twin of the sensor network** averaged 30 readings per window. Three questions sit under those choices: *what do samples miss or invent?* (sampling and aliasing), *what does averaging do to signal and noise?* (moving average), and *what does a one-line smoothing filter cost in lag?* (exponential filter). `labs/L500/24-sampling-filters/foundations2.py` checks each theorem, partly on data drawn from the stub's own sensors.

### Sampling theorem and aliasing (Shannon 1949, Theorem 1)
**In plain words** If a signal has no frequencies above W, samples taken 2W times a second capture it completely. If it *does* have higher frequencies, they do not disappear — they show up disguised as lower ones.

**Why it is true (intuition)** A band-limited spectrum fits in a finite window [−W, W], and anything on a finite window can be written as a Fourier series; the samples turn out to be exactly that series' coefficients. Aliasing is simpler still: two cosines whose frequencies differ by a multiple of fs agree at every sample instant, so nothing downstream can tell them apart.
1. Expand X(f) on [−W, W] as a Fourier series in f with period 2W.
2. The inverse transform shows each coefficient is x(n/2W)/(2W): the samples determine X, hence x.
3. Substituting back and integrating gives the sinc interpolation formula.
4. Aliasing: e^{j2π(f−k·fs)n/fs} = e^{j2πfn/fs}·e^{−j2πkn}, and the last factor is 1.
5. Choosing k = round(f/fs) puts the alias in [0, fs/2].

**See it in numbers** `python3 foundations2.py`:
```
OK   T1 sampling: W=4 Hz, fs=10 Hz, sinc reconstruction max error 0.0002 mid-record; 100 Hz sampled at 30 Hz equals 10 Hz to 8.2e-13; stub light FFT peak at 10.0 Hz (predicted |f - round(f/fs)fs| = 10 Hz)
```
A 4 Hz-limited signal sampled at 10 Hz (> 2 × 4) is rebuilt to 0.0002 in the middle of the record (the ends suffer from truncating the infinite sum). A 100 Hz flicker sampled at 30 Hz is numerically identical to 10 Hz (8.2e-13 is rounding), and the FFT of light readings from the stub finds the peak exactly at the predicted 10.0 Hz.

**Where it shows up in practice** Mains-powered lamps flicker at 100 Hz; a light logger sampling at a few hertz logs a slow "real" flicker that never existed.

### Moving average: frequency response and sqrt(N) noise reduction
**In plain words** Averaging the last N samples passes slow changes, cancels frequencies at exact multiples of fs/N, and cuts independent noise's standard deviation by √N.

**Why it is true (intuition)** A sinusoid that completes a whole number of cycles inside the N-sample window sums to zero. Independent noise adds in variance, not amplitude: N samples add N·σ², and dividing by N² leaves σ²/N.
1. Feed e^{jωn}; the output is e^{jωn}·H(ω) with H(ω) = (1/N)Σe^{−jωk}.
2. Sum the geometric series and factor out half-angle exponentials: |H| = |sin(Nω/2)/(N sin(ω/2))|.
3. The numerator is 0 at ω = 2πk/N (f = k·fs/N); the denominator is not.
4. Var(Σw_k/N) = Nσ²/N² = σ²/N, because covariances of independent samples vanish.

**See it in numbers**
```
OK   T2 moving average: N=16 closed-form |H| matches direct sum to 5.8e-16; stub accel z noise std ratio 3.98 (sqrt(N) = 4.00); N=3 at 30 Hz nulls 10 Hz: aliased flicker std 70.7 -> 0.00
```
The closed form agrees with brute-force summation to machine precision. On stub accelerometer noise a 16-point average reduced the std by 3.98, against √16 = 4. And a 3-point average at 30 Hz puts its first null at 30/3 = 10 Hz — exactly where the aliased flicker landed — so it vanishes (70.7 → 0.00).

**Where it shows up in practice** The twin's 30-reading windows in lab 20: a window mean is ~√30 ≈ 5.5× quieter than one reading.

### Exponential filter: step response, time constant, noise ratio
**In plain words** `y += α·(x − y)` moves a fraction α toward each new sample. Its step response is 1 − (1 − α)ⁿ⁺¹, its time constant is about Ts/α, and it reduces white-noise variance by α/(2 − α) — the same as an N-point average when α = 2/(N + 1).

**Why it is true (intuition)** Each sample's influence decays by (1 − α) per step, so the filter is a geometric weighting of the past. Sum the weights for the step; sum their squares for the noise.
1. With β = 1 − α the impulse response is h[n] = α·βⁿ.
2. Step: Σα·βᵏ = 1 − βⁿ⁺¹ (geometric series).
3. Remaining error βⁿ = e⁻¹ at n = −1/ln β, so τ = −Ts/ln(1 − α) ≈ Ts/α.
4. Noise: σ²·Σh² = σ²α²/(1 − β²) = σ²·α/(2 − α).
5. With α = 2/(N + 1): α/(2 − α) = 1/N.

**See it in numbers**
```
OK   T3 EMA: alpha=0.2, Ts=0.1 s -> step error vs 1-(1-a)^(n+1) 2.2e-16, tau 0.448 s (Ts/alpha = 0.500 s), -3 dB at 0.3566 Hz; stub light noise variance ratio 0.1120 (alpha/(2-alpha) = 0.1111, same as N=9 MA)
```
The exact τ is 0.448 s; the rule of thumb Ts/α says 0.500 s (α = 0.2 is not "small"). On stub light noise the variance ratio is 0.1120 against 0.1111 = 1/9: α = 0.2 behaves like a 9-point average for noise.

**Where it shows up in practice** Any smoothing added to lesson 14's logger: the lag is per *sample*, so at 8 s intervals τ becomes about 36 s.

## Worked problems
**24.1 — 100 Hz flicker sampled at 30, 45 and 60 Hz.**
1. Use f_alias = |f − round(f/fs)·fs|. *Why:* theorem 1 picks the alias in [0, fs/2].
2. 30 Hz: round(3.33) = 3 ⇒ |100 − 90| = 10 Hz.
3. 45 Hz: round(2.22) = 2 ⇒ |100 − 90| = 10 Hz.
4. 60 Hz: round(1.67) = 2 ⇒ |100 − 120| = 20 Hz. *Why it matters:* each looks like a genuine slow flicker in the log.

**24.5 — EMA with α = 0.2 at Ts = 0.1 s.**
1. τ = −0.1/ln(0.8) = 0.448 s. *Why:* exact formula from step 3, not the approximation.
2. Equivalent N = (2 − α)/α = 9. *Why:* invert α = 2/(N + 1).
3. Noise std ratio = √(1/9) = 0.333. *Why:* variance ratio 1/9, take the root.
4. −3 dB: cos ω = (1 + 0.8² − 2·0.2²)/(2·0.8) = 0.975 ⇒ ω = 0.2241 rad/sample ⇒ f = ω/(2π·0.1) = 0.3566 Hz. *Why:* set |H|² = ½ in the theorem's formula.

## Common confusions
**Q:** A digital low-pass after sampling removes aliasing. — **A:** After sampling the alias *is* a 10 Hz signal; filter before sampling or sample faster.

**Q:** Averaging N samples always gives √N. — **A:** Only for independent noise; correlated noise reduces less.

**Q:** τ = Ts/α exactly. — **A:** 0.448 s vs 0.500 s at α = 0.2.
