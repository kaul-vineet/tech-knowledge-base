# Reading seminar: Shannon (1949) and Liu & Layland (1973) — explained

## The big picture
Lessons 24 and 25 gave you two results in textbook form: the sampling theorem and the rate-monotonic utilisation bound. Both come from short papers that are still read because they *defined the question*. Reading the originals matters for one practical reason: the papers state their **assumptions** plainly, and assumptions are exactly where micro:bit projects break. The 100 Hz flicker that became a 10 Hz "signal" in **Foundations II: sampling and signal processing** breaks Shannon's band-limit assumption; the 24 ms button response in **Foundations III: control and real-time theory** breaks Liu & Layland's preemption assumption, and both trace back to labs you ran — **Sensing temperature and light** and **Non-blocking firmware structure**.

This guide does not reproduce the papers (both are copyrighted by IEEE and ACM; use the DOI links through a library). It tells you what to read first, what to skip on a first pass, three ideas to take away, and how each connects to the labs.

**How to read either paper (one pass each):** *Frame* — title, abstract, figures, conclusion. *Claims* — each result in your own words with its assumptions. *Evidence* — follow one argument end to end and mark the steps you would check with code. *Transfer* — where it touches your labs, and which assumption the micro:bit violates.

## Reading guide 1 — Shannon, "Communication in the Presence of Noise" (Proc. IRE, 1949)
**Read first**
1. The opening description of a communication system as a chain of elements (source, transmitter, channel with noise, receiver, destination). Map it onto lesson 7: the radio hardware is the channel; your `encode()`/`decode()` and lab 12's CRC and ARQ are the transmitter and receiver logic you wrote.
2. The sampling theorem (his Theorem 1) and the short argument that a band-limited function is fixed by samples 1/(2W) apart. Lesson 24's proof follows the same idea.
3. The geometric picture: a signal of bandwidth W and duration T becomes a point in roughly 2TW dimensions.
4. The capacity result for white Gaussian noise, C = W·log₂(1 + P/N).

**Skip on the first pass** The more technical derivations around the geometric bounds and the extended discussion of how ideal systems trade bandwidth for signal-to-noise ratio. Come back once the capacity formula makes sense.

**Three ideas to take away**
1. **Samples are coordinates.** Band-limiting turns a continuous signal into a finite list of numbers; that is what lets digital systems exist at all.
2. **Capacity is a limit on *any* code.** It bounds what is possible over a channel; it says nothing about what a particular radio achieves.
3. **Ideal performance costs delay.** Approaching capacity needs ever longer codes — a cost a sensor that must report within seconds cannot pay.

**See it in numbers** — problem 27.1 recomputed, and lesson 24's aliasing check:
```
27.1: ['3.459', '3.459 Mbit/s']
OK   T1 sampling: W=4 Hz, fs=10 Hz, sinc reconstruction max error 0.0002 mid-record; 100 Hz sampled at 30 Hz equals 10 Hz to 8.2e-13; stub light FFT peak at 10.0 Hz (predicted |f - round(f/fs)fs| = 10 Hz)
```
10 dB is a power ratio of 10, so C = 1 MHz × log₂ 11 = 3.459 Mbit/s — an upper bound for that channel, not a radio's data rate. The second line is the assumption failing: the stub's light signal was *not* band-limited below 15 Hz, so 100 Hz landed at 10 Hz.

**How it connects to the labs** The flicker alias (lab 5's light sensor, lesson 24), PWM harmonics (lab 9), and the radio's practical limits (labs 7 and 12). The assumption to question: no real sensor is strictly band-limited; prevention needs an analog filter or faster sampling before decimation.

## Reading guide 2 — Liu & Layland, "Scheduling Algorithms for Multiprogramming in a Hard-Real-Time Environment" (J. ACM, 1973)
**Read first**
1. The task-model assumptions near the start: periodic requests, deadline equal to period, independent tasks, constant run time, preemption. Write them as a checklist — you will need it.
2. The critical-instant theorem (their Theorem 1): the worst response comes when a task is released together with all higher-priority tasks. This turns an infinite schedule into one finite check.
3. The argument that rate-monotonic priority is optimal among fixed-priority assignments (an exchange argument: swapping two out-of-order priorities never hurts).
4. The utilisation bound n(2^(1/n) − 1) and its limit, about 70 %.

**Skip on the first pass** The full derivation of the n-task bound (lesson 25 gives the n = 2 case and the skeleton) and the later material on mixed scheduling. Read the deadline-driven result's statement, not its proof.

**Three ideas to take away**
1. **Worst case is constructible.** The critical instant tells you exactly which schedule to simulate.
2. **The bound is sufficient, not necessary.** Harmonic periods reach 100 % (lesson 25's check: "harmonic U=1 meets").
3. **Dynamic priorities can use the whole processor** — at the cost of run-time bookkeeping on every release.

**See it in numbers** — problem 27.2 and lesson 13's task set:
```
27.2: ['5(2^{1/5} - 1) = 0.7435', 'ln 2 = 0.6931']
lesson 13 U = 0.37 | n=3 bound = 0.7798
```
Five tasks may use 74.35 % of the CPU with a guarantee; the limit 0.6931 is the paper's "about 70 %". Lesson 13's three tasks use 37 %, comfortably under 0.7798 — yet lesson 25's check found a 24 ms button response against a 20 ms period, because the cooperative scheduler cannot preempt.

**How it connects to the labs** Lesson 13's scheduler violates *preemption* (tasks run to completion) and *constant run time* (radio retries and garbage collection vary); lesson 16's interrupt shows what you get when you do add preemption: shared-state races. The fix in analysis is a blocking term; the fix in design is shorter task budgets.

## Worked problems
**27.1 — Shannon capacity for W = 1 MHz, SNR 10 dB.**
1. Convert dB to a power ratio: 10^(10/10) = 10. *Why:* the formula uses P/N, not decibels.
2. log₂(1 + 10) = log₂ 11 = 3.459. *Why:* bits per second per hertz.
3. C = 10⁶ × 3.459 = 3.459 Mbit/s. *Why:* multiply by bandwidth; it is a ceiling for any coding scheme.

**27.4 — two broken assumptions.**
1. Preemption → lesson 13's cooperative loop. *Why:* a running task cannot be interrupted, so blocking adds to response time (19 ms + 5 ms = 24 ms).
2. Constant run time → lesson 12's retries and MicroPython garbage collection. *Why:* the bound uses one Cᵢ per task; a variable run time must be bounded by its worst case.

## Common confusions
**Q:** Shannon's capacity is what the micro:bit radio achieves. — **A:** It is an upper bound for an ideal Gaussian channel with unlimited coding delay.

**Q:** U above the bound means deadlines will be missed. — **A:** It means the test is inconclusive; simulate from the critical instant.

**Q:** Reading the paper means copying its proofs into notes. — **A:** Paraphrase, quote at most a phrase with a page reference, link the DOI.
